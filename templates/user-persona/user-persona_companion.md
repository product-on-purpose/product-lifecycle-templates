# User Persona: the companion

The reference behind the template. Read this when you want to know *why* a section exists, what the
research actually says, and where the standards and the product-management literature disagree with each
other.

## 1. Orientation

A user persona exists to say who a product is being built for, grounded in research rather than in
invention. Nielsen Norman Group states the discipline plainly: "Personas must be based on user research to
accurately represent a product's users" [[6]](#ref-6). No single canonical definition sentence could be
verified for this bundle: the three books a reader would most expect this type to cite, Cooper's *The
Inmates Are Running the Asylum* chapter 9, *About Face*, and Pruitt and Adlin's *The Persona Lifecycle*,
could not be retrieved as full text, and this companion states nothing about their prose beyond what a
publisher's own front-matter sample and a contemporaneous academic paper record
[[4]](#ref-4)[[34]](#ref-34)[[35]](#ref-35)[[36]](#ref-36). What is recorded about the method's origin comes
from Cooper's own later essays and from Pruitt and Grudin's 2003 paper, which quotes the 1999 book directly
[[1]](#ref-1)[[2]](#ref-2)[[3]](#ref-3).

At a glance:

- Cooper built the method to narrow a design team's focus onto specific people rather than a vague averaged
  user, then explicitly declined to keep control over how the term is used: "That community owns and is
  responsible for the tools of their trade, and they must assure their definition" [[2]](#ref-2). A bundle
  appealing to Cooper's authority for current practice is appealing to an authority its holder formally gave
  away.
- Of the 33 sources this research read in full, 20 are practitioner and 7 vendor, leaving 3 primary, 2
  academic and 1 standards. The persona is a practitioner artifact whose canon is thin: only two
  peer-reviewed papers could be read, and they do different jobs, one extending the method
  [[3]](#ref-3) and one arguing it cannot be tested at all [[19]](#ref-19).
- The sharpest finding is a design rule, not a warning: a persona's population coverage shrinks
  combinatorially as attributes accumulate, worked through to roughly 134 people in the United States for a
  21-attribute example [[19]](#ref-19). Every field a template adds makes the persona describe fewer real
  people.
- The famous 900 percent persona statistic traces to a single uncontrolled case study that bundled persona
  work with a full site redesign, a content overhaul, and an email-automation change, with no comparison
  group and no isolation of the persona variable [[22]](#ref-22). It supports nothing about personas and
  must not appear in this bundle as fact.
- Product-management literature is split rather than hostile or silent, which is different from what this
  research found for `business-case`. Klement argues for replacement [[23]](#ref-23), Nielsen Norman Group
  names and rebuts that position [[24]](#ref-24), Cagan publishes a how-to with no argument either way
  [[27]](#ref-27), and Teresa Torres's canonical discovery material never mentions personas at all
  [[25]](#ref-25).

## 2. Origins and evolution

Cooper's own account is that interviews on a single project produced three clusters, differentiated by
goals, tasks, and skill level, and he built three named archetypes to represent them: "So I created Chuck,
Cynthia, and Rob. These three were the first true, Goal-Directed, personas" [[1]](#ref-1). The stated
purpose was to let a design be argued from a specific person's point of view instead of the designer's own
or a vague averaged user [[1]](#ref-1). He later described the discipline in one word: "we tightly
restricted the number of personas we used" [[2]](#ref-2). Whether Cooper invented the persona outright or
formalised something already circulating is recorded as unsettled in this bundle's research log rather than
decided here. Neither account was tested against the other in this
research, and the disagreement is recorded rather than settled.

The publisher's free sample of *The Inmates Are Running the Asylum* confirms where the persona material
sits, Part IV, Chapter 9, book pages 123 to 148, and its table of contents lists subsection titles including
Design for Just One Person, The Elastic User, Be Specific, Hypothetical, Precision Not Accuracy, Personas
End Feature Debates, and It's a User Persona Not a Buyer Persona [[4]](#ref-4). The sample stops at front
matter: those are chapter-heading titles only, not chapter prose, and this bundle states nothing about the
argument beneath them.

**A second lineage split, not a modern one, opened almost immediately.** Pruitt and Grudin, writing from
Microsoft in 2003, quote Cooper dismissing heavier ongoing research as "sandpaper... Very expensive and
time-consuming, it wasn't solving the fundamental problem," and set their own position against it: "Unlike
Cooper, we feel strongly that Persona use needs to be complemented with a strong, ongoing effort to obtain
as much quantitative and qualitative information about users as possible" [[3]](#ref-3). Cooper later
characterised the Microsoft lineage far less kindly, in an essay explicit that he no longer defines the term
for anyone: "At Microsoft, they invented personas to defend the features that the engineers cooked up in
their ivory towers," with "hundreds of personas, one for each feature they wanted to inflict on their
users" [[2]](#ref-2). Both camps are named and sourced here, and neither is presented as settling the other.

## 3. Anatomy (section by section)

### Who They Are (lean and full)

The profile-led identity block is the format's near-universal baseline: five of the six published formats
this research read in full carry it [[5]](#ref-5)[[6]](#ref-6)[[7]](#ref-7)[[8]](#ref-8). Its content is
consistent across sources: a name, role or job description, and a short representative quote, "Persona
name," "Persona role," "Job description," "Persona quote" [[7]](#ref-7); "Name, age, gender, and an image of
the persona, preferably including some context in the background" [[8]](#ref-8); "Quotes to sum up the
persona's attitude" [[6]](#ref-6). The one candidate structure that discards this spine entirely, the Buyer
Persona Institute's 5 Rings of Buying Insight, is a different artifact for a different question, the buying
decision rather than product use, and is addressed in Relationships below rather than folded in here
[[9]](#ref-9).

### Goals and Motivations (lean and full)

Every format this research read in full carries a goals field in some form: "Professional goals" and a
field literally labelled "Motivatiors" in the source [[7]](#ref-7); "Any goals, attitudes, and concerns they
would have when using your product or service" [[8]](#ref-8); the government service-manual format's own
ordered list carries the same element [[5]](#ref-5). Nielsen Norman Group's baseline claim governs this
section as much as it governs the whole document: the goals recorded here must come from user research, not
from what a team assumes a user wants [[6]](#ref-6).

### Pains and Barriers (lean and full)

Every format read pairs a goals field with an obstacles field: Atlassian's "Challenges" [[7]](#ref-7), the
Interaction Design Foundation's "Needs" [[8]](#ref-8). This is the section most exposed to the equity
critique this research found: naming, imagery, and accessibility are three concrete bias vectors in how
pains and barriers get written, and a persona that never names a disability-related barrier is making a
choice, not an omission, given that nearly 40 million Americans reported a disability in the source year
cited [[21]](#ref-21).

### Context of Use (full only)

**This section is a build-spec departure, on evidence.** The original spec proposed a standalone
"Behaviors" section, and **no format this research read carries one under that name**. The closest published
match is the government service manual's Behaviour and preferences block [[5]](#ref-5); elsewhere the same
material is folded into a broader situational block, Usage Context in one source and Situations in another
[[6]](#ref-6)[[8]](#ref-8). The
section design follows the core the sources actually share rather than inventing a section to satisfy the
spec as originally written.

### Scenarios (full only)

Only the Interaction Design Foundation names Scenarios as its own labelled section, and it defines the
structure precisely: "The scenario should have a beginning (presents the user and what the persona wants to
achieve), middle (what the user does with your product and the persona's motivation for pursuing the goal)
and end (describes whether the persona succeeds in his or her intentions)" [[8]](#ref-8). Elsewhere the same
material is folded into Usage Context [[6]](#ref-6) or omitted [[7]](#ref-7). This is a genuine minority
practice among the sources read, not an absent one, which is why it ships as its own full-only section
rather than being merged into Context of Use.

### Evidence Basis (full only)

**This section is a build-spec departure, on evidence, and it is the section the honest-retrieval standard
cares about most.** The spec proposed a bounded "Quotes/Evidence" section; no format read in full carries
one, except where verbatim buyer voice is the entire document, which is the Buyer Persona Institute's
different artifact, not this one [[9]](#ref-9). What every format instead needs, and what this section
supplies, is a record of how much research actually sits behind the persona. Nielsen Norman Group names
three tiers by evidence level: proto personas built with "no new research" in a two to four hour workshop;
qualitative personas "based on small-sample qualitative research," with 5 to 30 interviews; and statistical
personas needing "at least 100 (ideally 500 or more) respondents" analysed by clustering
[[15]](#ref-15). A companion piece from the same publisher warns "Unfortunately, there isn't a golden
number" and pushes back on the five-per-persona heuristic [[16]](#ref-16). Both are Nielsen Norman Group.
Gothelf's proto-persona practice shows what the lightest tier looks like honestly labelled: personas built
from what executives believed, explicitly stated as "not going to be research-proven customer archetypes,"
used as filters and continuously updated against real user interactions [[12]](#ref-12)[[14]](#ref-14).
Gothelf and Seiden's own proto-persona work proposes two layouts for this lightest tier, a four-box and a
three-box format, though the page recording them was too sparse to yield a verbatim definition beyond the
naming itself [[13]](#ref-13). This
section exists so that a persona's evidence tier is a declared field a reader can check, not an unstated
assumption.

## 4. Variants and sizing

**Lean** carries Who They Are, Goals and Motivations, and Pains and Barriers: enough to name a specific
person and ground a design conversation in someone other than the designer. **Full** adds Context of Use,
Scenarios, and Evidence Basis, the three sections a reader needs to see how the persona actually behaves and
how much research stands behind the picture. One format ships, under both weights, per the format verdict in
`user-persona_research-log.md`.

Three candidate formats were considered and did not ship as separate structures. The **proto-persona** is
not structurally distinct from the shipped format; what differs is the evidence behind it, not its sections,
so it is taught in the Evidence Basis section and carried as a declared field rather than a second outline
[[12]](#ref-12)[[14]](#ref-14). The **buyer persona**, built on the 5 Rings of Buying Insight, genuinely
replaces the profile spine outright and answers a different question, the purchase decision rather than
product use; it is out of scope, and the boundary is taught rather than shipped [[9]](#ref-9)[[29]](#ref-29).
The **anti-persona** is addressed by two sources; the one that speaks to its packaging builds and publishes
it as a separate artifact with its own data, "created data-based just like a buyer persona, only 'the other
way around'" [[10]](#ref-10), and no source read presents it as a section inside a persona
[[32]](#ref-32). Folding it into `full` would teach a shape no read source practises, so it is named in the
Relationships section below as a sibling document a team may choose to write, on a departure recorded in the
research log's contested register as one source's packaging evidence rather than a refutation.

## 5. Methodology lineage

This type leans design thinking, and the lineage is Cooper's goal-directed design as extended and disputed
by the practitioners who followed him. The founding disagreement never closed: Cooper's own position, as
quoted by Pruitt and Grudin, is that a lighter initial investigation suffices, while theirs is that persona
use needs a strong, ongoing research effort [[3]](#ref-3). Nielsen Norman Group's three-tier evidence ladder
formalises both ends of that disagreement into one instrument, naming the no-research tier and the
statistical tier as legitimate points on the same scale rather than declaring one the correct method
[[15]](#ref-15).

**The evidence base behind the whole method is uneven rather than absent, and one study measured the
unevenness.** Salminen and colleagues extracted 130 knowledge claims from 346 persona research articles and
report a higher degree of consensus on what a persona is against a high proportion of unverified claims in
the creation and use categories, concluding the field carries "claims that are not substantiated with strong
empirical evidence and warrant future work" [[20]](#ref-20). This was read from a project-authored summary
rather than the primary publication, which limits what may rest on it, and the companion 2022 systematic
review could not be retrieved at all [[37]](#ref-37).

## 6. Debates and contested boundaries

**Did Cooper invent the persona, or formalise something already circulating?** Pruitt and Grudin note the
abstract user was a known problem and that comparable ideas, including Geoffrey Moore's Target Customer
Characterizations, were in circulation [[3]](#ref-3). Cooper's own account presents it as an original
discovery on a specific project [[1]](#ref-1). Neither was tested against the other here.

**How much research must sit behind a persona?** Cooper's position is that a lighter initial investigation
suffices and heavier ongoing research "wasn't solving the fundamental problem"; Pruitt and Grudin's is that
persona use needs a strong and ongoing research effort [[3]](#ref-3). This is the field's founding
disagreement.

**Is a persona a profile or a decision narrative?** Four of the formats read organise around identity, goals
and pains [[5]](#ref-5)[[6]](#ref-6)[[7]](#ref-7)[[8]](#ref-8). The Buyer Persona Institute discards that
spine for the 5 Rings of Buying Insight and argues the buyer's own voice is the document: "pictures don't
talk, and it's her voice that you need" [[9]](#ref-9). This is a lineage split between UX and B2B marketing,
not a quality difference.

**Does Scenarios belong as a labelled section?** Only the Interaction Design Foundation names it as one,
defining it structurally as beginning, middle and end [[8]](#ref-8). The others fold the same material into
Usage Context [[6]](#ref-6), or omit it [[7]](#ref-7).

**Is the anti-persona a section or a separate document?** The one source addressing its packaging publishes
it separately [[10]](#ref-10); the other defines the concept without saying how it is packaged
[[32]](#ref-32). This bundle follows the packaging source and departs from its own build spec, on one
source. A second source either way would settle it.

**Is there a minimum interview count?** Nielsen Norman Group gives 5 to 30 for qualitative personas
[[15]](#ref-15) and, in a companion piece, warns "Unfortunately, there isn't a golden number" and resists
the five-per-persona rule [[16]](#ref-16). Both are the same publisher.

**Do personas improve design outcomes?** The two sources that examined the evidence base as a whole conclude
the rigorous evidence for creation and use claims is thin or absent [[19]](#ref-19)[[20]](#ref-20). The
circulating counter-evidence is a single uncontrolled case study that isolated nothing [[22]](#ref-22). The
honest answer is that nobody has shown it either way.

**Is persona failure a method problem or an implementation problem?** Flaherty attributes failure entirely
to organisational process, buy-in, siloed creation and poor communication, and does not question the
method's validity [[18]](#ref-18). Chapman and Milham attribute it to the method itself, independent of
implementation quality [[19]](#ref-19). These are different diagnoses with different remedies, and a reader
should know which one they are buying.

**Is JTBD meant to replace personas?** Klement argues yes and proposes a structurally different artifact
[[23]](#ref-23). Nielsen Norman Group names that position and rebuts it as compatible rather than competing
[[24]](#ref-24).

**How often should a persona be revised?** Nielsen Norman Group surveyed the cadence and found it spread
rather than clustered: "nearly half of respondents (46%) update personas every 1-4 years"
[[17]](#ref-17), with the rest divided between much more frequent and much less frequent revision. Only the
event triggers are unambiguous; the cadence is not.

## 7. Anti-patterns and failure modes

**Treating an unresearched persona as though it were evidence-backed.** Gothelf is explicit that his own
proto-personas were "not going to be research-proven customer archetypes" [[12]](#ref-12); the failure is
not building one, it is presenting it as more than what it is.

**Adding attributes past what changes a decision.** The curse of dimensionality is a design rule, not an
abstract warning: a persona's population coverage shrinks combinatorially as attributes accumulate, worked
through to roughly 134 people in the United States for a 21-attribute example [[19]](#ref-19). Every field
added makes the persona describe fewer real people, and each one should earn its place.

**Citing the 900 percent statistic, or figures like it, as fact.** It resolves to a single uncontrolled case
study that bundled persona work with a full site redesign, a content overhaul, and an email-automation
change, with no comparison group and no isolation of the persona variable [[22]](#ref-22). The number is
real; it supports nothing about personas.

**Treating the persona as an isolated UX deliverable.** Flaherty's account of why personas fail is
organisational rather than method-level: "Personas should not be an isolated endeavor undertaken by the UX
team and unveiled like a piece of artwork," not "8.5 x 11 handouts" nobody uses [[18]](#ref-18).

**Building a persona to justify a decision already made.** Chapman and Milham cite prior empirical studies
for the finding that persona use served primarily to justify decisions made on other grounds; this bundle
attributes that finding to Chapman and Milham citing that work, not to the underlying studies directly,
since those studies were not independently retrieved [[19]](#ref-19).

**Letting personas go stale.** Nielsen Norman Group names concrete triggers, business change, competitive
change, and user-base drift, and states plainly that a persona untouched for five or more years is
"performing for your products about as well as a dull butter knife cutting steak" [[17]](#ref-17).

**Writing pains and barriers that quietly exclude disability.** Naming, imagery, and accessibility are three
concrete bias vectors this research found named directly, against a stated population this research does not
independently verify beyond the source's own figure [[21]](#ref-21).

**Letting a persona harden into a marketing segment wearing a person's name.** Nielsen Norman Group names
this pattern directly as "marketing segments being masqueraded as personas" [[24]](#ref-24), a different
failure from unfalsifiability: the document still looks like a persona, but the research behind it has been
replaced by a market category.

## 8. Relationships to other artifacts

**What this document is not, and what stays separate from it.** It is not a **buyer persona**: a buyer
persona reveals insight into the buying decision, "the attitudes, concerns, decision criteria, and journey
that drive prospective customers to choose you, your competitor or the status quo," where a profile that
only lists individual characteristics reveals nothing about how to influence that decision
[[29]](#ref-29). It is not an **anti-persona**: the one source addressing its packaging builds and publishes
it as its own artifact, "created data-based just like a buyer persona, only 'the other way around'", a
sibling document a team may choose to write once the positive persona exists, not a section inside it
[[10]](#ref-10)[[32]](#ref-32). It is not a **market segment**: a segment groups an addressable population
by shared measurable traits, where "Personas feel like real people while representing a bunch of customers.
A market segment is more about a faceless crowd" [[30]](#ref-30). It is not an **empathy
map**: the map is a single-session, five-sense workshop canvas for understanding a stakeholder in a specific
context, and it is a structurally different artifact from this document. Its source does not name personas
at all, so nothing here rests on a claim about how the two are used together [[33]](#ref-33). And the **proto-persona is not a different artifact from this one**: it is an evidence tier
of the same document, assumption-built and explicitly labelled that way, updated as research arrives, which
is why it lives in the Evidence Basis section here rather than shipping as a second format
[[12]](#ref-12)[[14]](#ref-14)[[15]](#ref-15).

**What is downstream of it.** Once a persona exists, it is the upstream research artifact a team writes
agile user roles and user stories from: "Personas offer a great way to capture the users and the customers
with their needs," feeding the sprint-ready requirement written from the persona's perspective
[[31]](#ref-31). Within this library's own discovery-docs family, the persona is the second of three
documents that answer, in order, whether an investment is worth making, who it is being built for, and what
to build cheaply first to test that; `prototype-brief` is the member that takes over once a persona exists
and a team is ready to test a specific hypothesis about the person this document describes.

**An open debate about whether this document is being replaced rather than merely bounded.** Klement argues
personas should be replaced outright by a different artifact built on anxieties and purchase-progress events
[[23]](#ref-23); Nielsen Norman Group names that position and argues the two artifacts, persona and
jobs-to-be-done, answer different questions, who versus what, and are complementary rather than competing
[[24]](#ref-24). Teresa Torres's canonical account of the opportunity solution tree, the structural core of
continuous-discovery practice, never mentions personas anywhere [[25]](#ref-25); that is an absence observed
in a document that was read, not an inference about her views, and it is stated that way rather than as a
verdict.

## 9. Adaptations

**Teams with a government or regulated service-manual tradition** should expect a dated, periodically
reviewed persona rather than a one-time artifact, following the same document-review discipline the source
service manual practices [[5]](#ref-5), and should apply Nielsen Norman Group's named revision triggers,
business change, competitive change, and user-base drift, rather than a fixed calendar
[[17]](#ref-17).

**Executive-led organisations without a research budget** should recognise Gothelf's proto-persona pattern
by name rather than build an unlabelled imitation of a research-backed persona: personas built openly from
what executives believe, explicitly not research-proven, and continuously updated as real user interactions
surface new detail [[12]](#ref-12)[[14]](#ref-14). Nielsen Norman Group's evidence ladder makes clear which
tier that is, and a team should say so on the document rather than let a reader assume otherwise
[[15]](#ref-15).

**B2B and enterprise sales-facing teams** should check whether they actually need a buyer persona before
reaching for this template: the 5 Rings of Buying Insight answer a purchase-decision question this document
does not [[9]](#ref-9)[[29]](#ref-29). The same teams should also keep an ideal customer profile separate
from either: an ICP "defines the perfect company for your product or service," a firmographic filter on the
organisation, while a buyer persona describes "an individual decision-maker within your ideal customer
companies," and neither substitutes for a user persona describing how someone actually uses the product
[[28]](#ref-28).

**Teams building for a broad or public user base** should treat disability inclusion as a deliberate choice
in the Pains and Barriers section rather than an afterthought, given the concrete bias vectors this research
found named directly in naming, imagery, and accessibility [[21]](#ref-21).

**Teams already practising jobs-to-be-done or continuous discovery** should read the actual debate rather
than assume replacement is settled either way: one camp argues for outright replacement
[[23]](#ref-23), the leading practitioner counter-source argues for compatibility
[[24]](#ref-24), and continuous discovery's own canonical material is simply silent on the question rather
than taking either side [[25]](#ref-25).

See `user-persona_example.md`. Per this library's discovery-docs family contract, the example defines the
Recurring Analyst, the person the `kpi-dashboard` metric definitions and the `acceptance-criteria` example
