# Companion: Product Vision

> The reference document for the `product-vision` bundle. It explains what a product vision is, where the
> circulating formats came from, how it differs from the five artifacts it is most often confused with, and
> what actually goes wrong. Read section 1 before filling anything in; use section 3 as the per-section deep
> dive the templates point into.
>
> Every non-obvious claim is cited. Sources, retrieval status and quotable phrases are in
> [`product-vision_research-log.md`](product-vision_research-log.md); **only phrases that log marks as
> verbatim from a fetched-and-verified source are quoted here.**

## 1. Orientation

A product vision says what future this product is trying to create, for whom, and why this team is the one to
build it.

**The test is not how it reads. The test is whether it can refuse something.** The sharpest formulation any
source offers is Aakash Gupta's: *"If your vision can't kill a feature request from an influential
stakeholder, it isn't doing its job"* [[15]](#ref-15). A vision that has never been cited to say no is
decoration, however well written. That is why the lean template carries **What This Rules Out** as one of only
four sections rather than holding it back for the full variant: it is the section that makes the other three
usable.

**Expect the failure mode to be disuse, not bad writing.** Janna Bastow states the pattern plainly: *"Most
product vision statements get written once, pinned to a Notion page, and never read again"*
[[14]](#ref-14). Nothing in a template prevents that. What a template can do is make the vision short enough
to remember and specific enough to argue with.

**One evidence-backed piece of craft advice exists, and it is worth more than the rest.** Carton, Murphy and
Clark found that vision language carrying a large amount of concrete **imagery** together with a **small**
number of values is associated with better performance, and that leaders in practice tend to do the inverse,
under-using imagery and over-using value-laden rhetoric [[30]](#ref-30). Concrete picture, few abstractions.
Read the caveats in section 6 before leaning on it: that study examined leader **rhetoric** rather than
written vision documents, only its experimental half is causal, and the finding reaches this bundle as
abstract-level metadata rather than from the paper itself.

**This bundle ships three formats, because the field genuinely disagrees about whether a template can do this
job at all.** See section 4.

## 2. Origins and evolution

This subject has no standards body and no specification. Its canon is books, blogs and named practitioners,
which is why **misattribution rather than staleness is its characteristic failure mode** (section 6). What
follows is what could actually be verified.

**Roman Pichler published the Product Vision Board in May 2011** and has maintained it since
[[5]](#ref-5). It is the clearest structured-template tradition for product vision specifically. Pichler
names Alexander Osterwalder's Business Model Canvas and Ash Maurya's Lean Canvas as tools in the same space
[[5]](#ref-5), and his own enumeration of the board's five parts is unambiguous: *"The vision captures the
ultimate purpose for offering a product. The target group characterises the product's users and customers.
The needs describe the problem the product should address or the benefit it should offer. The product section
states its standout features. The business goals capture the desired benefits the product should achieve for
the company developing and providing it."* [[6]](#ref-6) Secondary sources that describe the board as having
four cells are counting the four supporting cells and omitting the vision cell [[19]](#ref-19).

**Marty Cagan is the primary named practitioner writing about the product vision as such**, across SVPG
articles from 2016 [[3]](#ref-3) to 2020 [[1]](#ref-1)[[2]](#ref-2) and 2024 [[4]](#ref-4). His position is
that the vision is *"a bit of an art form, as fundamentally it is a persuasion tool"* [[1]](#ref-1), that
*"A good product vision inspires ordinary people to create extraordinary products"* [[1]](#ref-1), and that
it may take the form of *"a story board, or a narrative like a white paper, or a prototype (referred to as a
'visiontype')"* [[3]](#ref-3).

**Three widely repeated attributions do not survive checking, and this bundle avoids all three.**

- **Collins and Porras wrote about companies, not products, and the usual citation order is backwards.**
  *Built to Last* was published 1994-09-16 [[10]](#ref-10); the Harvard Business Review article *Building Your
  Company's Vision* appeared in the September-October **1996** issue [[9]](#ref-9). The article is a later
  distillation of the book, not its origin. The framework's scope is organisational: the book's own listing
  describes its subject as *"their ultimate creation is the company itself"* [[10]](#ref-10). BHAG, core
  ideology and envisioned future were borrowed into product work by others. The HBR article is paywalled and
  was not read [[9]](#ref-9).
- **The positioning template attributed to Geoffrey Moore could not be verified.** *Crossing the Chasm* was
  first published in 1991 [[12]](#ref-12). The fill-in-the-blank statement universally credited to it was not
  found verbatim in any source read across two research passes, and no source identified a chapter, page or
  edition. It may well be in the book; its wording and location simply cannot be asserted. Independently of
  that, a positioning statement answers a different question than a vision does (section 8), so this bundle
  neither reproduces it nor ships it as a format.
- **Nobody names the originator of the Amazon PR/FAQ.** *Working Backwards* (Bryar and Carr, 2021-02-09) is
  the book-length account [[11]](#ref-11), and the authors' own site attributes the practice to Amazon
  collectively without crediting an individual [[32]](#ref-32). The practice also predates the product-vision
  literature and was designed to define a specific launch rather than a multi-year direction, so using it as
  a vision format is a practitioner extension.

**One attribution improved on checking.** The aphorism "be stubborn on the vision, and flexible on the
details" circulates widely and is credited to Jeff Bezos, including by Cagan, who quotes it as
*"stubborn on the vision, and flexible on the details"* [[3]](#ref-3). Amazon's 2020 letter to shareholders,
Bezos's last as CEO, contains it in his own words: *"On the details, we at Amazon are always flexible, but on
matters of vision we are stubborn and relentless."* [[31]](#ref-31) That is the form worth quoting. The
condensed versions in circulation remain of unverified provenance; the 2016 through 2019 and 2021 letters
were each checked and do not contain the phrase [[31]](#ref-31).

## 3. Anatomy (section by section)

The canvas format's sections, in template order. Lean carries the first three plus **What This Rules Out**.

**The Vision.** The future you intend to create, as a state of the world rather than a plan. This is where
the imagery finding applies most directly [[30]](#ref-30). Pichler's guidance is that it should be *"short and
sweet"* and *"easy to memorise and recite"* [[7]](#ref-7); ProdPad's practical test is that it *"can be
repeated from memory in under 30 seconds."* [[34]](#ref-34) The most common error is writing a mission here
instead (section 8).

**Who It Is For, and What They Need.** Pichler's board separates the target group from the needs for a
reason: the group names who the product is for, and the need names the durable problem it addresses
[[6]](#ref-6). A vision for everyone constrains nothing, and a vision that constrains nothing cannot decline
anything.

**Why Us.** What makes this team the one that reaches the future. Cagan's framing of the vision as a
persuasion tool [[1]](#ref-1) applies here more than anywhere: without a reason to believe, the document
describes a future anyone could pursue.

**Market and Competitive Context** (full only). What people use today, and why those alternatives are
structurally unable to reach this future.

**What This Rules Out.** The section the document is judged by [[15]](#ref-15). Two to five concrete
refusals, at least one of which a reasonable colleague would argue for. If every exclusion is comfortable,
the section is theatre.

**Business Goals** (full only). What reaching this future is worth to the organisation, in its terms. This is
the fifth cell of Pichler's board [[6]](#ref-6), and it is the guard against **borrowed grandeur**, Bastow's
name for the case where *"a startup writes a vision way bigger than its actual ambition"* [[14]](#ref-14).
Keep numeric targets out: those are key results and will be stale long before the vision is.

**Horizon and Review** (full only). Practitioner guidance on horizon clusters loosely and disagrees at the
edges: Cagan writes that *"The product vision should describe the desired end state 2-5 years out for software
companies, and 5-10 years out for device companies."* [[2]](#ref-2), while Pichler asks for guidance
*"for at least the next five years"* [[8]](#ref-8), so Cagan's software ceiling is Pichler's floor. Neither
offers evidence. The most useful framing found is Mezzatesta's: *"A one-year vision is not visionary - it is
a roadmap. A ten-year vision risks becoming disconnected from market reality."* [[18]](#ref-18) The review
trigger matters more than the number, because disuse is the dominant failure [[14]](#ref-14). Pichler
suggests inspecting the board *"at least once every three months as a rule of thumb"* [[6]](#ref-6), which is
his rule for his own tool rather than an industry norm. Critically, a strategy change is not a vision change:
*"a strategy change does not necessitate a vision change"* [[8]](#ref-8).

**Leaps of Faith** (full only). An ambitious multi-year vision cannot be validated before commitment. Mind
the Product puts it as *"a vision can't always be fully validated upfront - it requires a leap of faith"*
[[17]](#ref-17). Naming the unproven parts, and what would disprove them, is what makes the review trigger
actionable and what separates a considered bet from optimism.

## 4. Variants and sizing

**Two axes, and they are independent.** Size is how much you fill in; format is what shape the document
takes. This is the first bundle in the library to use both.

**Sizes.** Lean is four sections for a team that needs a shared destination it can cite. Full adds the four
sections a vision needs to survive people who were not in the room.

**Formats, and the disagreement that forced three of them.** The two practitioners who wrote most directly
about product vision disagree about whether a fillable template can do this job. Cagan is explicit:
*"don't expect to find a simple fill-in-the-blanks, paint-by-numbers, canvas or board approach to a strong
product vision"*, because *"A strong product vision is the result of a creative process, much like crafting a
story."* [[2]](#ref-2) Pichler has shipped exactly such a board since 2011 [[5]](#ref-5).

**This library is a template library, so shipping only a canvas would have taken a side while quoting the
objection.** Instead it ships three:

| Format | Shape | Reach for it when |
|---|---|---|
| **canvas** (default, lean and full) | a filled grid, one page | you need to orient people fast and be citable in a prioritisation argument |
| **narrative** (full) | prose, present tense, two to four pages | the vision has to make someone *want* the future, not merely understand it |
| **prfaq** (full) | a launch announcement dated years out, plus its FAQ | the live question is whether this future is worth having at all |

They are siblings, not sizes: none of their outlines is a subset of another, and the gate deliberately
asserts no nesting relationship between them. Cagan's own alternative to a written vision is the
**visiontype**, a prototype that dramatises the future; he argues *"The power of a compelling visiontype is
that it helps you to imagine a future that could be."* [[4]](#ref-4) The narrative format is the closest
written analogue this bundle can teach.

**The fourth circulating shape, the positioning sentence, is deliberately not shipped.** See section 2.

## 5. Methodology lineage

**The product vision is methodology-agnostic, and the more useful fact is that most agile frameworks do not
define it at all.** Checked directly:

- The **Scrum Guide** (2020) does not contain the word "vision"; its nearest concept is the Product Goal
  [[23]](#ref-23).
- The **Nexus Guide** does not either, verified by reading the full document rather than a summary
  [[24]](#ref-24).
- The **Agile Manifesto** and its twelve principles do not [[25]](#ref-25).
- **SAFe** does define a Vision as an explicit artifact [[26]](#ref-26), and **Scrum at Scale** names a
  Strategic Vision owned by the Chief Product Owner [[27]](#ref-27).

**This vacuum explains the rest of this companion.** A Scrum team has no standard telling them to write a
vision, what it should contain, or when it is done. Practitioner books and blogs filled that gap, which is
why the canon is named individuals rather than a specification, and why attributions drift the way section 2
documents.

## 6. Debates and contested boundaries

**Inspiration versus structure.** Cagan argues for persuasion and against canvases [[1]](#ref-1)[[2]](#ref-2);
Pichler ships a structured board [[5]](#ref-5)[[6]](#ref-6). No empirical comparison exists. This bundle
declines to pick a winner and applies the decision-utility test to both [[15]](#ref-15).

**What the evidence actually says, stated carefully.** There is a real research literature, and it is not
about product vision documents. **A retrieval caveat governs all three studies below and travels with every
claim drawn from them:** each was reached through abstract metadata rather than by reading the paper, so what
follows is design and reported finding at abstract level, and no phrase from any of them is quoted anywhere in
this bundle.

- Baum, Locke and Kirkpatrick studied 183 entrepreneur-CEO and employee pairs longitudinally in a single
  industry, assessed visions against seven attributes, and reported that vision content affected subsequent
  venture growth both directly and through communication [[28]](#ref-28). One industry, longitudinal but not
  experimental, and the outcome is venture growth rather than product outcomes.
- Kirkpatrick and Locke ran a lab experiment with 282 business students, and reported that high-quality
  vision affected attitudes strongly but performance only weakly, while **vision implementation**, meaning
  concrete task guidance, was the stronger driver of performance [[29]](#ref-29). Students on a simulated
  task are a long way from a product team.
- Carton, Murphy and Clark combined an archival study of 151 hospitals with an experiment on 62 groups of
  employees, and reported the imagery-and-few-values result described in section 1 [[30]](#ref-30). That
  study examined leader rhetoric rather than written documents, and only the experimental half is causal.

**Three honest consequences.** No study found measures a product-level vision document against product-team
outcomes. Every real-organisation study is observational, so practitioner writing that says vision *causes*
performance is upgrading a claim the studies do not make. And the paper most often cited in this space,
Bart and Baetz 1998, is about **mission** statements, is paywalled, and was not read [[21]](#ref-21).
**This bundle therefore claims no performance benefit for writing a product vision.**

**Ownership is contested three ways**, and the honest answer depends on the organisation: Scrum-oriented
writing puts it with the Product Owner, collaboratively [[22]](#ref-22); enterprise practice puts it with
product leadership, as Product School states, *"The CPO, VP of Product, or someone in Product Leadership is
responsible for defining the Product Vision and communicating it."* [[20]](#ref-20); and Cagan argues against
per-team visions entirely, because *"When each team has their own vision, it's the equivalent of everyone
picking out their own star from the sky, and calling it their 'north star' and then heading in their own
direction."* [[1]](#ref-1)

**Misattribution is this subject's characteristic hazard**, and the worked example is the Saint-Exupery ship
quote. It is used constantly in vision writing and it appears in **none** of his published work; the nearest
real passage is *Citadelle* section LXXV, thematically related and wholly different in wording, and the
earliest traceable English instance is a November 1999 Usenet post where it is already misattributed
[[33]](#ref-33). It appears nowhere in this bundle.

## 7. Anti-patterns and failure modes

1. **Wallpaper.** Written once, stored, never read again [[14]](#ref-14). The dominant failure. Defended
   against by a named review trigger and by being short enough to recite [[7]](#ref-7).
2. **A vision that refuses nothing.** If it cannot kill a plausible request from someone senior, it is not
   guiding anything [[15]](#ref-15).
3. **Aspirational fluff.** Gupta's example: *"'Delight users through innovation' sounds nice and helps nobody
   decide what to build"* [[15]](#ref-15). The imagery finding is the constructive form of this warning
   [[30]](#ref-30).
4. **Borrowed grandeur.** A vision larger than the team's actual ambition or resources [[14]](#ref-14).
   Business Goals is the guard.
5. **A roadmap wearing a vision's clothes.** Features and dates in the vision. Bastow's separation is clean:
   *"A product vision is how you choose a destination. The roadmap is how you plan the route."*
   [[14]](#ref-14)
6. **A mission mislabelled as a vision.** The most common confusion of all, per Cagan: *"Most people I meet,
   when they show me their 'product vision,' what they are really showing me is their 'mission statement.'"*
   [[1]](#ref-1)
7. **Per-team visions.** Each team picking its own star [[1]](#ref-1).
8. **Rewriting the vision whenever the strategy changes.** If the destination moves every time the route
   does, it was never a destination [[8]](#ref-8).

## 8. Relationships to other artifacts

**Versus mission.** Every product management source read agrees on direction: vision faces the future,
mission the present. LogRocket puts mission as *"defined in the present tense, as it tries to address what
the product is trying to actively achieve"* against vision as *"a futuristic, long-term view of the company
and product's objective"* [[16]](#ref-16), and notes the two are *"often used interchangeably"* in practice
[[16]](#ref-16). Sources disagree about which question each answers, and Bastow offers the most practical
resolution: *"The label matters less than whether the statement actually orients the team toward a
destination."* [[14]](#ref-14) **A caution for readers arriving from outside product management:** adjacent
management writing sometimes uses the two words the other way round. Product management practice does not.

**Versus strategy. Settled, with no dissent found.** Cagan: *"the product vision describes where we
ultimately want to go; the product strategy helps us decide what problems to solve in order to get to our
vision."* [[3]](#ref-3) Herbig: *"Product Strategy describes the set of choices needed to achieve your Product
Vision."* [[13]](#ref-13) The vision should be strategy-agnostic.

**Versus roadmap.** Destination against route [[14]](#ref-14).

**Versus north star.** Two different things share the phrase. As a metaphor, "north star" describes what a
vision does. As an artifact, a **North Star Metric** is a distinct quantitative thing; Herbig's formulation
keeps them apart, calling it *"the North Star Metric, which acts as the quantified sibling of the Product
Vision"* [[13]](#ref-13).

**Versus positioning statement.** **No product management source read draws this boundary**, which is
notable given that the positioning template is simultaneously the most circulated "vision template"
(section 2). Drawn from first principles, and labelled as this bundle's own reasoning: a positioning
statement says how this product stands against alternatives for a specific customer **now**; a vision says
what future is being created. One is competitive and present-tense, the other aspirational and future-tense.

**Versus company vision.** A matter of scope rather than kind. Product School notes that large multi-product
companies give different products distinct visions [[20]](#ref-20); in a single-product company the two may
simply be the same document [[13]](#ref-13).

## 9. Adaptations

- **Single-product startup.** Company vision and product vision are likely the same document
  [[13]](#ref-13). Write one. Lean canvas format, and keep the horizon honest.
- **Multi-product organisation.** Large multi-product companies give different products distinct visions
  [[20]](#ref-20). Cagan's warning about per-team visions applies to teams within one product, not to
  distinct products [[1]](#ref-1).
- **Small team, or a solo product.** **No source read addresses whether a vision is worth writing at this
  scale**; the practitioner literature assumes medium-to-large organisations. Judge it by the decision-utility
  test rather than by alignment benefits that only appear at scale.
- **Hiring or fundraising.** Use the narrative format. Cagan notes the vision is *"one of our most effective
  recruiting tools"* [[3]](#ref-3).
- **A vision nobody uses.** Do not rewrite it first. Find out whether anyone has ever cited it to decline
  something; if not, the problem is in **What This Rules Out**, not in the prose.
