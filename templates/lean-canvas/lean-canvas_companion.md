# Lean Canvas: the companion

The reference behind the template. Read this when you want to know *why* a section exists, what the
research actually says, and where the record is thin or argued rather than sourced.

## 1. Orientation

A Lean Canvas states a product's business model on one page, in nine boxes, as beliefs to test rather than
as a finished plan [[6]](#ref-6)[[15]](#ref-15). Ash Maurya built it by adapting Alexander Osterwalder's
Business Model Canvas "for startups operating under conditions of extreme uncertainty"
[[7]](#ref-7). It helps a founder "deconstruct an idea into key assumptions or beliefs"
[[6]](#ref-6) and is revised as those beliefs are tested, not written once and filed [[15]](#ref-15).

At a glance:

- **A hypothesis sheet, not a business plan.** The canvas is contrasted with a business plan on how long
  each takes to write: "While a business plan can take weeks or months to write, your initial canvas
  should be sketched quickly." [[15]](#ref-15)
- **Fast and short by design.** "your initial big idea canvas should be sketched quickly - in less than 20
  minutes" [[6]](#ref-6), on one page: "Embrace a 1-page constraint." [[6]](#ref-6)
- **Drafted alone first, then reconciled.** "If you're part of a team, avoid creating a Lean Canvas as a
  group exercise. Instead, have each team member create their snapshot first." [[6]](#ref-6)
- **Built for uncertainty, not for an established line of business as such.** Maurya's own company draws
  the line at product-market fit: "If you are pre-product-market fit, start with Lean Canvas."
  [[13]](#ref-13)
- **This library places it second** in the `strategy-docs` chain, between the product vision and the
  product strategy, and names that placement a POSITION rather than a finding; see Debates, below.
- **It is copyrighted.** No open licence appears on Maurya's own pages. This companion names the boxes and
  quotes him briefly with attribution; it does not reproduce the canvas's visual layout.

## 2. Origins and evolution

**A 2010 blog post, built on a worksheet and two canvases.** Ash Maurya published the canvas on his own blog on
2010-08-12, in a post titled "How I Document My Business Model On 1 Page" [[1]](#ref-1). He worked from
Steve Blank's customer-development worksheets, then met Alexander Osterwalder's Business Model Canvas and
found it wanting for his purpose: "I do find some of Osterwalder's blocks a bit too general for a lean
startup" [[1]](#ref-1). After meeting Rob Fitzpatrick's own adaptation of that canvas, he wrote, "So I
decided to iterate on this one more level and created a version that looks like this" [[1]](#ref-1). His
2011 walkthrough tells the same lineage in its own words: he "found some of his canvas elements too
general" and made "an adaptation that eventually became the Lean Canvas" [[15]](#ref-15).

**It was tested in public before it was software.** "Lean Canvas was tested and refined in these
presentations and workshops before it ever became an online tool" [[15]](#ref-15), including a session at
Capital Factory's Demo Day in Austin, in front of a mostly investor audience [[15]](#ref-15). Maurya's
current pages state the authorship plainly: "Lean Canvas was created by me, Ash Maurya, and is an
adaptation of Alexander Osterwalder's Business Model Canvas - optimized for the Lean Startup methodology."
[[6]](#ref-6)

**He kept Osterwalder's layout on purpose, to credit the source.** "I chose to work within the existing
layout constraints to attribute the work back to Alex Osterwalder's original canvas" [[2]](#ref-2). His own
retrospective explains the boxes he changed: he judged some of the boxes he removed more "outside-in" than suited to a founder working alone [[2]](#ref-2). Of customer relationships specifically he wrote, "This seemed better captured by the
existing Channels box." [[2]](#ref-2) Of key partners he wrote that for an unproven startup, "pursuing key
partnerships from day one can be a form of waste." [[2]](#ref-2) The frame behind the whole redesign is
Eric Ries's: "Startups operate under conditions of extreme uncertainty." [[2]](#ref-2) This companion does
not pair those two reasons into a claim about exactly which two boxes he meant by "outside-in": no quoted
line names the pair directly, and the source material does not support going further than his own two
separate explanations.

**Four Business Model Canvas boxes were replaced by four new ones.** Maurya's current pages describe the
swap directly: he replaced key partnerships, key resources, key activities and customer relationships with
problem, solution, key metrics and unfair advantage [[2]](#ref-2)[[6]](#ref-6)[[7]](#ref-7). The original
2010 post shows this as it stood then, not as it stands now: its fifth box was still headed "Key Activity":
"5. Key Activity: Describe the key action users take that maps to revenue or retention?" [[1]](#ref-1) So
the first published version removed three Osterwalder boxes, added three, and repurposed the fourth; the
box that is now Key Metrics was Key Activity in 2010. The 2024 teaching page asks for three to five outcome
metrics in that box [[6]](#ref-6); treat the renaming as a change over time, not as the box's original
job.

**Maurya's own account of his timeline is not repeated here beyond what is directly quoted.** His
retrospective gives month-level dates for his own exposure to Osterwalder's book and to Fitzpatrick's
variant, and for the online tool's start [[2]](#ref-2), but those dates sit awkwardly against a separate
line on the same page about when he first built Business Model Canvases of his own [[2]](#ref-2), and the
underlying research found that summary unreliable. This companion states only what the Quotable lines
above directly support and does not give a month-by-month timeline or *Running Lean*'s first edition year.

**Maurya's material now sits on several of his own sites.** "LEANSTACK is the company behind LEANSpark."
[[4]](#ref-4) The teaching pages, the product guide and the comparison page used throughout this companion
live on leanspark.ai [[6]](#ref-6)[[7]](#ref-7)[[8]](#ref-8)[[13]](#ref-13). His blog archive, including the
2010 post, lives on ashmaurya.com, which also names the current edition of his book, *Running Lean*, as the
third, of 2022 [[3]](#ref-3). His company bio on LeanFoundry calls him "Creator of Lean
Canvas" [[5]](#ref-5). A 2024 post on a third address, leanspark.leanstack.com, is discussed in Debates,
below [[11]](#ref-11). No page read announces the move between these addresses; that the material has
moved is inferred from where it now sits, not stated by Maurya directly.

## 3. Anatomy (section by section)

The template ships one size. Every section below is present in it; the order follows Maurya's own current
teaching page, which starts from the customer [[6]](#ref-6).

### Customer Segments

**What it is.** Who has the problem and who will pay for a solution to it, with a line inside the box for
Early Adopters.

**Why it exists.** Maurya's product page defines the box and its sub-block directly: "Customer Segments -
Who has the problem and who will pay" and "Early Adopters - The specific subset of customers you target
first" [[7]](#ref-7). The 2024 teaching page draws the customer/user line sharply: "A customer is someone
who pays for your product. A user does not." [[6]](#ref-6)

**Beginner note.** Start here. Both the current teaching page and the 2011 walkthrough begin the fill
order from this box [[6]](#ref-6)[[15]](#ref-15), and the walkthrough ties it to the next box directly: "The
'Problem-Customer' pair usually drives the rest of the canvas which is why I tackle them together."
[[15]](#ref-15) Write the early adopter, not the eventual mainstream buyer: "Your objective is to define an
early adopter, not a mainstream customer." [[6]](#ref-6)

**Expert note.** If more than one segment is genuinely in play, the 2011 method sketches a separate canvas
per segment and compares them side by side afterward, prioritized by customer pain, ease of reach, price and
market size in that order [[15]](#ref-15), reported here in paraphrase; the guide's Picking a variant
section carries the same note. The 2024 page does not repeat that rule in its
own numbered guidelines [[6]](#ref-6), so treat it as an available method from the originator's own
writing, not as his current foregrounded instruction.

### Problem

**What it is.** The top one to three problems this segment has, with a line inside the box for Existing
Alternatives.

**Why it exists.** "List the top one to three problems." [[6]](#ref-6) Maurya's reasoning for keeping the
box narrow is explicit: "Problems, not solutions, create spaces for innovation." [[6]](#ref-6) The
Existing Alternatives sub-block asks a separate question: "Document how you think your early adopters
currently address these problems today." [[6]](#ref-6)

**Beginner note.** An alternative does not have to be a named competitor. Maurya's 2011 text gives the
example of email standing in as the biggest alternative to a collaboration tool, and notes that doing
nothing at all can be an alternative too [[15]](#ref-15).

**Expert note.** Draft this box together with Customer Segments, not after it: the pairing is deliberate,
because together they "drive the rest of the canvas" [[15]](#ref-15).

### Unique Value Proposition

**What it is.** The single message that states why this product is different and worth attention, with a
line inside the box for the High-Level Concept, an "X for Y" analogy.

**Why it exists.** The High-Level Concept is defined directly: "The X for Y analogy that makes your idea
instantly understandable." [[7]](#ref-7) Maurya's 2011 walkthrough places the Unique Value Proposition
itself at the centre of the canvas and calls it among the most important and hardest boxes to get right;
no line of his on this point is Quotable, so this companion reports it in paraphrase rather than quoting
him [[15]](#ref-15).

**Beginner note.** This is one of the thinnest-sourced boxes in the canvas: Maurya's own description of
why it is hard to write survives only as a paraphrase, not a direct quotation, so treat any stronger claim
about it with caution.

**Expert note.** In the 2011 text the High-Level Concept reads as a side exercise, a few analogies jotted
down, rather than a canvas entry in its own right [[15]](#ref-15); the current page names it as one of the
twelve blocks outright, the nine boxes plus three named sub-blocks [[7]](#ref-7). Treat the stronger status as the 2024 teaching, and the lighter
treatment as how Maurya described it earlier.

### Solution

**What it is.** The simplest thing that addresses each listed problem, in a box kept deliberately small.

**Why it exists.** "I purposely made the solution box less than one-ninth of the entire canvas"
[[6]](#ref-6). The instruction to wait is stated twice, in both eras of his writing: "Bind a solution to
your problem as late as possible." [[6]](#ref-6)[[15]](#ref-15)

**Beginner note.** Fill this box after Customer Segments and Problem are settled, not before. Writing a
solution early is the specific habit the rest of this companion calls the solution-first trap; see
Debates, below.

**Expert note.** The box's small size on the page is itself part of the argument: a founder who finds the
box too small to hold a full solution description is being told something about scope, not about
formatting.

### Channels

**What it is.** The path to reach customers, now and at scale.

**Why it exists.** This is the thinnest-sourced box in the bundle. Maurya's 2011 text advises selling
manually before automating, and selling in person before letting others sell on the founder's behalf; no
line of his on this point survives as a direct quotation, so it is reported here as paraphrase
[[15]](#ref-15).

**Beginner note.** Where this box is thin on texture, lean on the current teaching page's box order
[[6]](#ref-6) rather than inventing detail the research did not find sourced.

**Expert note.** No source read names a channel strategy framework that extends this box; treat it as a
short, founder-written line rather than a place for a full go-to-market plan.

### Revenue Streams

**What it is.** How the product makes money, priced now rather than modeled years out.

**Why it exists.** "Rather than thinking in terms of three- or five-year forecasts, it's better to take a
more stage-based approach. Focus on your most immediate short-term milestones three to six months from
now." [[6]](#ref-6) On timing: "I believe pricing is one of the riskiest (and most critical) parts of the
business model and should be tested early." [[15]](#ref-15)

**Beginner note.** Write a near-term price and a near-term milestone, not a multi-year revenue model. The
2011 case study works a short back-of-the-envelope break-even from the cost to launch, rather than a
formal forecast [[15]](#ref-15).

**Expert note.** This box shares its sourcing with Cost Structure below: the same stage-based-approach
quotation covers both boxes in the current teaching [[6]](#ref-6).

### Cost Structure

**What it is.** What it costs to reach the next milestone, three to six months out, not a multi-year
budget.

**Why it exists.** The same stage-based instruction that governs Revenue Streams governs this box: "Focus
on your most immediate short-term milestones three to six months from now." [[6]](#ref-6)

**Beginner note.** Pair this box with Revenue Streams when drafting; the 2011 worked case study treats
cost and break-even together [[15]](#ref-15).

**Expert note.** Like Channels, this box has thin sourcing beyond the one shared quotation and the one
worked example; do not over-read structure into it that the source material does not carry.

### Key Metrics

**What it is.** Three to five outcome metrics that show the business model is working.

**Why it exists.** "List 3-5 key metrics" [[6]](#ref-6), and the current teaching states a clear
preference: "Prefer outcome metrics versus output metrics." [[6]](#ref-6) The page offers "Typical SaaS
metrics" as worked examples of the kind of metric meant [[6]](#ref-6).

**Beginner note.** This box has changed name and content over time; see Origins, above. Fill it to the
current, 2024 instruction (three to five outcome metrics) rather than to the box's 2010 wording, which
asked about a single key activity instead [[1]](#ref-1).

**Expert note.** This box was renamed from Key Activity to Key Metrics after 2010 [[1]](#ref-1); a reader comparing an older worked example against this
template should expect the box's content, not just its heading, to differ.

### Unfair Advantage

**What it is.** Something about the business that cannot be easily copied or bought.

**Why it exists.** "A real unfair advantage is something that cannot be easily copied or bought."
[[6]](#ref-6)[[15]](#ref-15). The definition is Jason Cohen's, and both of Maurya's pages quote it under
his name [[6]](#ref-6)[[15]](#ref-15). The box's purpose is forward-looking rather than a pass/fail test: it exists "to
continually encourage you to work towards finding/building your unfair advantage" [[2]](#ref-2).

**Beginner note.** It is normal to leave this box blank at first: "Leave the unfair advantage blank."
[[6]](#ref-6) The 2011 walkthrough calls it the hardest section to fill and places it last in the
recommended order for exactly that reason [[15]](#ref-15).

**Expert note.** This is one of the richest-sourced boxes in the canvas: Maurya addresses it directly in
both the 2011 walkthrough and the 2024 teaching page, in near-identical wording, which is unusual among the
nine boxes.

### Riskiest Beliefs and Next Tests

**What it is.** A record of which beliefs on this canvas are weakest, what evidence (if any) backs them,
and what test comes next.

**Why it exists.** This section is this library's own addition to the nine boxes Maurya publishes; no
source read publishes a canvas template with a tenth section of this kind. What it records is sourced
directly to Maurya's own teaching, layered on top of the nine boxes rather than inside them: "focus on your
weakest links first - your riskiest assumptions" [[11]](#ref-11), checking first whether "customer and
problem assumptions are not yet grounded in empirical learning" [[11]](#ref-11). His product page frames
the whole canvas around this discipline: "identifying and testing your riskiest assumptions before you
build" [[7]](#ref-7), and his company's comparison page states it even more bluntly: "Find the riskiest
assumption, test it." [[13]](#ref-13) The prompt for what comes next is his own question: "what are the
next hypotheses you need to test to move your product forward?" [[6]](#ref-6)

**Beginner note.** Name beliefs from the boxes above, by box (a Customer Segments belief, a Problem belief,
and so on), and say in one line what evidence, if any, currently backs each one. A canvas with every belief
marked "untested" is honest; a canvas that marks everything "validated" on day one is not.

**Expert note.** Treat this section as this library's own structural choice, built on a sourced practice,
not as a transcription of a box Maurya himself publishes. A reviewer should not expect to find this
section's heading on any of Maurya's own pages.

### Versions

**What it is.** A dated row per revision: what changed on the canvas and why.

**Why it exists.** Like the section above, this is this library's own addition, chosen to match the
`utility` classification this family contract gives the canvas: a standing instrument that earns its value
by being kept current, not a document finished once. The practice it records is sourced: the canvas is "an
organic document that evolves over time" [[15]](#ref-15), built for "tracking ongoing learning"
[[15]](#ref-15), and Maurya's own software tool now automates exactly this: "Your canvas is versioned
automatically. You can view past versions and restore any previous state." [[8]](#ref-8) The same tool also
supports parallel variants: "Your business model can have multiple variants - different pivot directions or
market segments you're exploring." [[8]](#ref-8)

**Beginner note.** Add a row whenever a belief in Riskiest Beliefs and Next Tests resolves, confirmed or
refuted, and say which box changed as a result.

**Expert note.** No source read names a missing change-log field as a complaint about the plain template;
this section is not filling a documented gap in Maurya's own design. It is this library's own choice for a
standing document, matching a feature his own current tool happens to offer in software rather than on
paper.

## 4. Variants and sizing

**One size ships: `lean`.** Every source that addresses length agrees the canvas is compact. "your initial
big idea canvas should be sketched quickly - in less than 20 minutes" [[6]](#ref-6); "Set a timer for 20
minutes." [[6]](#ref-6) The one-page limit is treated as a discipline, not a formatting shortcut:
"Describing your idea on a single page is not about using smaller fonts but fewer words." [[6]](#ref-6)
The 2011 walkthrough agrees from the other direction: "Sketch a canvas in one sitting" and "The boxes on
the Lean Canvas are intentionally small because it forces you to be concise." [[15]](#ref-15) Blanks are
allowed rather than padded out: "it's okay to leave boxes blank" [[6]](#ref-6), and "It's okay to leave
sections blank" [[15]](#ref-15). This library's own choice to ship one size, recorded in
[ADR 0083](../../docs/internal/decisions/0083-lean-canvas-joins-strategy-docs-as-a-seventh-member.md),
follows directly from that compactness rather than from a sourced claim that no longer variant exists
anywhere.

## 5. Methodology lineage

**The canvas is a Lean Startup artifact, and this companion did not find another lineage for it.** Its
redesign is framed explicitly around Eric Ries's premise: "Startups operate under conditions of extreme
uncertainty." [[2]](#ref-2) Maurya's own pages describe it as an adaptation of Osterwalder's Business Model
Canvas "optimized for the Lean Startup methodology" [[6]](#ref-6). This bundle's research did not
examine how any particular delivery methodology or regulated industry treats the canvas, so this
companion makes no claim about one. This
companion does not extend the canvas's lineage beyond Lean Startup, because nothing read supports doing
so.

## 6. Debates and contested boundaries

**Startup against established company: the sourced line is product-market fit, not company age.** Maurya's
pages draw the boundary by stage. His product page adapts the Business Model Canvas "for startups
operating under conditions of extreme uncertainty" [[7]](#ref-7). His company's own comparison page is more
direct still: "Lean Canvas was designed for entrepreneurs, not consultants, customers, advisors, or
investors" [[13]](#ref-13), and it calls the Business Model Canvas "the better tool for mapping and
communicating established business models" [[13]](#ref-13). Its working rule is explicit: "If you are
pre-product-market fit, start with Lean Canvas." [[13]](#ref-13) The same page also softens the line: "Use
what is most natural for you." [[13]](#ref-13) No source read argues the opposite case, that the canvas
suits an established, proven business better than a new one; this is Maurya's own company's framing, not
a debate between named camps.

**Canvas against business plan.** Maurya's walkthrough states the practical contrast directly: "While a
business plan can take weeks or months to write, your initial canvas should be sketched quickly."
[[15]](#ref-15) The original 2010 post carries a sharper pair of definitions: "Business Plan: A document
investors make you write that they don't read. Business Model: A single diagram that describes your
business." [[1]](#ref-1) Whether that pair of lines is Maurya's own coinage or one he is quoting from
elsewhere could not be established from the post itself, so this companion presents it as the 2010 post's
wording, not as a phrase original to him. No source read argues the other side of this contrast.

**The solution-first trap.** The canvas is built against it structurally: the solution box is deliberately
less than a ninth of the page [[6]](#ref-6), binding a solution to the problem is pushed as late as
possible [[6]](#ref-6)[[15]](#ref-15), and problems are placed ahead of solutions in the fill order
[[6]](#ref-6). A trade-press interview reports Maurya naming the habit directly as an "innovator's bias",
describing it as a love for the solution that he says he shares, and describing his own prescription as a
shift in focus from the product being built to the business model being built. That page is a journalist's
report of an interview, not Maurya's own writing, so this companion presents the material as reported
rather than as a direct quotation from him [[10]](#ref-10).

**The dropped boxes.** Maurya presents every one of the four boxes he removed as a deliberate choice, with
stated reasons [[2]](#ref-2)[[6]](#ref-6)[[7]](#ref-7). No named critic arguing the opposite, that the
removal weakens the canvas, was found in this bundle's research. One adjacent source exists but does not
serve as that critique: Roman Pichler writes that his own, different tool, the Product Vision Board, "does
not capture the business model including the competitors, the partners, the channels" [[12]](#ref-12)],
which is a remark about his own board's own limits, not a critique of Maurya's canvas.

**The "Leaner Canvas" question is resolved, not open.** An earlier sweep of this library's research found
pages describing a "Leaner Canvas" that it could not confirm against Maurya's own writing. A 2024 post of
his, "Reorder your Chain of Beliefs with a Leaner Lean Canvas", answers the question directly
[[11]](#ref-11). A full canvas, he writes, is "a great exercise for baselining an idea", but "at the
earliest stages, however, it helps to more narrowly focus attention on just the two outer boxes of the
canvas" [[11]](#ref-11), which are Customer Segment and Problem. His stated reason: "If your customer and
problem assumptions are not yet grounded in empirical learning, it's prudent first to get them in order."
[[11]](#ref-11) The "Leaner" canvas, in his own words, is a narrowing exercise performed on the same nine
boxes, not a second, separate template.

**What could not be settled.** No source read states a calendar interval for revising a canvas as a rule
(see Adaptations, below, on the one named exception). No source read compares the Lean Canvas by name with
a business case, a product strategy, a positioning statement, a North Star metric or the Lean UX Canvas.
This companion's Relationships section, below, says so plainly rather than inventing a comparison no source
makes.

## 7. Anti-patterns and failure modes

**Drafting the canvas as a group exercise.** Maurya's current teaching page names this directly and gives
the fix: "If you're part of a team, avoid creating a Lean Canvas as a group exercise. Instead, have each
team member create their snapshot first." [[6]](#ref-6) The individual snapshots are then shared, not
merged by committee: "The important thing is sharing it with at least one other person when you are done."
[[15]](#ref-15)

**Skipping the canvas altogether.** The 2011 walkthrough names this as a real and common failure, not a
hypothetical one: "A lot of entrepreneurs skip this step altogether which is NOT the solution."
[[15]](#ref-15) The same text frames the alternative as carrying the business model only in one's head:
"Too many founders carry their hypotheses in their heads alone which makes it hard to systematically build
and test a business." [[15]](#ref-15)

**Treating Revenue Streams and Cost Structure as a multi-year forecast.** The current teaching page names
the fix directly: "Rather than thinking in terms of three- or five-year forecasts, it's better to take a
more stage-based approach." [[6]](#ref-6)

**The solution-first trap.** Writing a solution before the problem and customer are settled is the specific
failure the canvas's layout is built to resist; see Debates, above, for the sourced material on this
pattern [[6]](#ref-6)[[10]](#ref-10)[[15]](#ref-15).

**Filling it once and treating it as finished.** The canvas is meant to be kept current, not archived after
one pass: "The canvas is meant to be an organic document that evolves over time" [[15]](#ref-15). A canvas
with no Versions entries after its first sketch is a sign this discipline has lapsed, not a sign the model
is settled.

theoretical limitation of the Business Model Canvas is the lack of incorporating strategy" [[14]](#ref-14).

states the business model, and `product-strategy` makes the choices that a model alone does not.

"the lean canvas" for that bundle's own lean-size `canvas` format, an unrelated meaning of the same two
words. A reader of both bundles should not confuse that short-form product-vision document with Ash
Maurya's Lean Canvas described in this companion; they are two different artifacts that happen to share a
name.

## 9. Adaptations

**Team size changes how the canvas is drafted, not what it contains.** On a team, each member sketches an
individual snapshot before any group reconciliation: "If you're part of a team, avoid creating a Lean
Canvas as a group exercise. Instead, have each team member create their snapshot first." [[6]](#ref-6) No
source read describes a different adaptation for a solo founder beyond simply skipping that step.

**A programme can impose a cadence the canvas itself does not require.** One named accelerator states its
own calendar rule: "The Lean Canvas will be a weekly deliverable for the Full Time cohort as part of our
weekly accountability framework." [[9]](#ref-9) It uses the weekly canvas "to check your progress and
identify issues to address" [[9]](#ref-9). That is a single cohort's own accountability policy, not a
property of the canvas, and this companion does not generalize it into a recommended rhythm for every
use.

**A new line of business inside an established company is an extension this companion names honestly, not
a sourced practice.** Maurya built the canvas for founders working under extreme uncertainty, and his
company's own test for when to reach for it is product-market fit, not company age or company size
[[7]](#ref-7)[[13]](#ref-13). A new, unproven line of business inside an otherwise established company sits
on the same uncertain side of that test. No source read in this bundle's research addresses that situation
directly, either to endorse using the canvas there or to argue against it; this companion states the test
Maurya's own sources give and leaves the extension to the reader's own judgment.

**No adaptation for a particular methodology or regulated industry is described.** This bundle's research
did not examine one, so this companion names no such adaptation rather than inventing one.

## 10. Worked example

[`lean-canvas_example.md`](lean-canvas_example.md) models a Lean Canvas for a new embedded-analytics OEM
line of business inside Acme, the library's continuing worked scenario. The product strategy example
already names this line a non-target for Acme's core product, requiring "a different product" sold to a
different customer; a new, unproven line inside an established company is the closest honest fit to
Maurya's own test of extreme uncertainty, stated above in Adaptations. The example shows at least two
dated entries in its Versions section, so that this bundle's `utility` classification is visible on the
page, not only argued in this companion.
