# Positioning Statement: the guide

An operator card. Read it before you fill the template and again before you agree the statement with
the team. The reasoning behind every rule here lives in
[`positioning-statement_companion.md`](positioning-statement_companion.md).

## When to use this document

Write it once three things are true: a product strategy already names the target segment this
statement is written for, the team can say what that target does today without the product, and the
positioning has already been worked out through analysis rather than guessed at on the page. The
document is then the dated, internal record of that analysis, written down for two reasons April
Dunford gives: "Once you have all the pieces together, you will want to write it down." Her two reasons:
a shared record of what the team agreed, and "The document is dated so you can track when the
positioning was last reviewed."

This library places positioning after the product strategy has already chosen a segment, because the
statement depends on that choice being made, not on which document gets written first in principle. That
placement is this library's own position, argued rather than sourced; see the companion's Debates
section before treating it as settled.

## Is this the right document at all?

| If the real question is | Write this instead |
|---|---|
| Where are we trying to get to, over years, and why is it worth it? | [`product-vision`](../product-vision/) |
| Which problems will we solve to get there, and which will we not? | [`product-strategy`](../product-strategy/) |
| What exact words will sales and marketing use with customers? | a messaging framework. This library has not yet admitted one; see the companion's Relationships section |
| What does a rep say when a named competitor comes up in a live deal? | a battlecard. Not yet admitted either, and downstream of this document, not an input to it |
| Which single measure of customer value shows the strategy is working? | [`north-star-metrics`](../north-star-metrics/) |
| In what order will we work on those problems? | [`product-roadmap`](../product-roadmap/) |
| What measurable change do we expect this period, and did we get it? | [`okrs`](../okrs/) |

## When NOT to use this document

- **The reader might be external.** Every source that names a reader names an internal one. HubSpot is
  direct about it: "your positioning statement isn't public-facing." If the audience is a customer, the
  press, or an investor, this is not that document, and April Dunford's own methodology is explicit that
  it is scoped to one kind of reader: "The methodology in Obviously Awesome is designed specifically for
  customer positioning-not investor positioning, not employee positioning, not partner positioning."
- **The analysis has not happened yet.** If nobody has worked out the target, the real alternative, and
  the differentiation, do not open this template to find out. Do that work first; this document records
  the result.
- **The question is where the product is headed, not how it wins today.** That is `product-vision`'s job.
  This document is present tense only.
- **The content is customer-facing language.** Talking points, a tag line, a sales script: that is
  messaging, built on top of this record once it exists, never a substitute for it.
- **The product strategy has not yet named a segment.** Positioning restates a segment the strategy has
  already chosen; writing it first would mean guessing at the segment instead of inheriting it.

## Picking a variant

There is nothing to pick. This bundle ships one size, `lean`, and every admitting source agrees on why.
MIT: a positioning statement "forces you to use a one page format to summarize everything that matters
for your product." HubSpot: "Aim for no more than three to five sentences, if possible." Yesware gives
the same three-to-five-sentence ceiling. April Dunford's own material is genuinely longer, a workbook she
gives her newsletter subscribers, but this library reads that workbook as a facilitation process for
reaching the one-page statement, not a second, heavier version of the statement document itself. Do not
invent a full-size version by copying her workbook's steps into this template; that content belongs in a
facilitation process, not in the standing record. See the companion's Variants and sizing section.

## Who owns this document

Two camps answer this question differently, and neither is this library's invention. Blackblot, Young
Urban Project, and Douglas Karr's account of the Pragmatic Framework all name product marketing as the
owner. Aha!'s own guide and blog, and Devika Nair's account of a product-management and product-marketing
split, instead describe the work as joint or shared rather than owned by one function alone. Which camp
fits your own company is, in this library's own reading, an organization-dependent question rather than a
settled one. No source states this library's own suggested default, that product management owns the
statement as product direction while product marketing drafts or co-writes it, and where the sources do
lean, they lean toward product marketing instead. The default is offered anyway, as this library's own
reasoning rather than a finding: this bundle is scoped to product direction, and the other `strategy-docs`
examples are owned by product leadership. What matters more than which default you pick is that the
document names one owner.

## The rubric

Score each row 0, 1 or 2. Below 10 out of 14, do not let this stand as the team's record yet: at least
one section still reflects a guess rather than analysis, or the document cannot tell a future reader
what to check or when to come back to it.

| # | Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Boundary names neighbors** | The Purpose and Boundary section lists the four exclusions in the template's own generic wording, with nothing about this product added | The exclusions are restated in product-specific terms, but a reader could not yet tell, from this section alone, which existing document already carries the content being excluded | Each exclusion names the artifact that actually carries that content for this product, so a reader who starts writing the wrong kind of sentence here can be pointed to where it belongs |
| 2 | **Statement follows components** | At least one word in the sentence, a benefit, a category, a claim, does not appear anywhere in the Components below it | Every word in the sentence traces to the Components below it, but the Components section still reads as justification written after the sentence rather than analysis that produced it | Every clause in the sentence traces to specific wording in a Component below it, and deleting the sentence would cost the reader nothing the Components did not already say |
| 3 | **Real rival, not phantom** | The named alternative is a category of competitor rather than one specific thing a customer would actually do, or it is a named competitor nobody on the team can point to a real, lost deal against | The alternative is specific, but the document does not say whether the team has actually lost to it or is guessing that customers would choose it | The alternative is specific, and the document states why the team believes it is the real rival: a named deal lost to it, or a stated reason the status quo is the default choice |
| 4 | **Differentiation survives substitution** | Swapping the named alternative's name in place of the product's name in the differentiation sentence would still sound credible; nothing in the wording is specific to this product | The differentiation names something specific to this product, but Why This Positioning offers no proof beyond the claim restated in different words | The differentiation fails the swap test, and Why This Positioning names a specific proof point, a measured result or a documented customer reaction, not the claim restated |
| 5 | **Target defined by need** | The target is a demographic or firmographic label with no stated need, or the need is a restatement of the product's own feature list | A need is named, but it is generic enough to describe almost any product in the category | The need is specific enough that a reader could recognize a real customer conversation from it, and the target is defined by having that need rather than by a label alone |
| 6 | **Revisit has an owner** | The section states only a calendar interval, with no named event that would call for an earlier look and nobody named to notice one | A trigger event is named, but nobody is named as responsible for noticing it, or the check-in interval is missing | A dated last review, a stated check-in interval, and a named trigger appear together, with somebody named to notice that trigger |
| 7 | **Separate from messaging** | The document states talking points, a tag line, or sales language directly, instead of the competitive analysis such language would be built on | The document avoids messaging language, but nothing in it says that messaging comes next or points to where that work happens | A reader can tell from the document alone that messaging, a battlecard, or a sales pitch is the next, separate piece of work built on top of it |

## Anti-patterns

- **Filling the sentence in from assumption instead of from the six components' own analysis.** This is
  April Dunford's central, repeated critique of the fill-in-the-blank exercise, not a minor stylistic
  complaint: "The only valuable thing we can learn from the statement is that there are multiple
  components of positioning." Writing the sentence first and reverse-engineering the Components to match
  it is the same failure in reverse.
- **Positioning against a phantom competitor.** April Dunford warns that a rival the team has never
  actually lost a real deal to "should not be considered when working on your positioning." Naming one
  anyway invites the team to defend against a threat that is not actually costing it business.
- **Treating the status quo as an afterthought.** Dunford's own account puts it first, not last: "the
  first competitive alternative we need to beat is the status quo." A document that jumps straight to a
  named competitor and never asks what the customer does today without the product has skipped the
  harder, more common case.
- **Importing a messaging cadence or trigger list into this record.** A messaging framework may carry its
  own quarterly review cycle; this document does not inherit it. The Starr Conspiracy states which
  document to settle first: "Lock the positioning statement first."
- **Writing it once and never referring to it again.** April Dunford names the specific failure a dated
  record exists to prevent: a document that is "completed and never, ever referred to again" has lost the
  only two things writing it down was supposed to buy the team.
- **Rewriting it every quarter.** A near-constant rewrite is a symptom, not a positioning problem, in
  CLIMB's own diagnosis: "If you find yourself rewriting your positioning statement every quarter, the
  problem likely isn't the statement. It's that the underlying strategic choices haven't been made firmly
  enough."
- **A differentiation claim that passes the substitution test.** If swapping the named alternative's name
  into the sentence in place of the product's own name would still sound credible, the claim is not yet
  specific enough. Lumen Learning's own desk test: "If you can substitute any competitor's name for your
  own in the positioning statement-and it still sounds credible-then you need some additional work on
  your differentiators and competitive advantages."
- **Crediting the formula to Geoffrey Moore.** No source this library's research read firsthand attributes
  the fill-in-the-blank form to him. Treat the attribution, if it comes up, as unverified, never as a fact
  to put in this document or its guidance.

## Before you share it

- [ ] You can point at the Components section and show every word of The Statement came from it.
- [ ] The named alternative is something specific, and you can say whether the team has actually lost a
      deal to it.
- [ ] The differentiation fails the substitution test: swap in the alternative's name and it stops
      sounding credible.
- [ ] Why This Positioning names a proof point, not just a restated claim.
- [ ] The revisit section has a date, an interval, and at least one named trigger with an owner.
- [ ] Nothing here is messaging, a battlecard, or a positioning map wearing this document's heading.
- [ ] You have deleted every HTML comment before sharing it.
