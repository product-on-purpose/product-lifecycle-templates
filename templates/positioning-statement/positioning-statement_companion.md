# Positioning Statement: the companion

The reference behind the template. Read this when you want to know *why* a section exists, what the
research actually says, and where the field disagrees with itself.

## 1. Orientation

A positioning statement names how this product stands against a named alternative, for a specific target
customer, now. It is a short, internal record of a competitive stance the team has already worked out
through analysis, not a public claim and not a messaging deliverable
[[2]](#ref-2)[[3]](#ref-3)[[4]](#ref-4)[[36]](#ref-36). The document names six components (the target, the
need, the category, the benefit, the named alternative and the differentiation) and then summarizes them
into one sentence, because the bare sentence, filled in from assumption rather than from the components
behind it, is the specific failure the field's own sharpest critic spent five posts attacking
[[7]](#ref-7)[[8]](#ref-8)[[13]](#ref-13).

At a glance:

- **Internal, not public-facing.** Every source that names a reader names an internal one, and two name
  outsiders only as secondary consumers of the same document
  [[2]](#ref-2)[[3]](#ref-3)[[4]](#ref-4)[[36]](#ref-36).
- **Present tense, not future tense.** It states why a customer chooses this product today, in the market
  as it exists today, not where the product is headed [[11]](#ref-11)[[23]](#ref-23)[[26]](#ref-26).
- **The named alternative is what the customer would otherwise do**, and the first one to beat is usually
  doing nothing at all, not a named competitor [[18]](#ref-18)[[53]](#ref-53).
- **Short, by every admitting source.** One page or three to five sentences, never a multi-page document
  [[1]](#ref-1)[[2]](#ref-2)[[4]](#ref-4)[[5]](#ref-5).
- **Revisited, by a dated check-in or a named trigger or both**, not left to run forever unread. Three
  sources state a cadence and a fourth asks for a dated record precisely so a team can tell when it was
  last reviewed [[8]](#ref-8)[[26]](#ref-26)[[30]](#ref-30)[[52]](#ref-52).
- **This library places it third** in the `strategy-docs` chain, between the product strategy and the
  North Star metric, and names that placement a POSITION rather than a finding; see Debates, below.

## 2. Origins and evolution

**Five sources carry a slot-based formula in readable text today, and no two are identical.** MIT's Orbit
Knowledgebase ships six fields: target, need, product, category, benefit and a named competitor
[[1]](#ref-1). HubSpot's five-field formula merges benefit and differentiation into one slot and closes on
a reason-to-believe clause [[2]](#ref-2). eCornell's #IMPACT formula names a "point of differentiation" and
a "frame of reference" in place of category and differentiation, and insists the wording may vary but "the
five main components" may not be dropped [[3]](#ref-3). Yesware's six-field formula adds an outcome clause
no other form carries [[4]](#ref-4). The classic form, as Heinz Marketing's summary of Dunford's first
edition renders it, carries a target, a product, a category, a benefit and a named competitor, with no
need or reason-to-believe slot [[9]](#ref-9). Product Marketing Alliance's own field-by-field template sits
behind a membership gate this research could not open, so none of its slots may be quoted
[[5]](#ref-5). **This library's own six-component list, target, need, category, benefit, named alternative
and differentiation, is a convergence across these forms, not any one source's exact wording**, following
the same posture `data-dictionary`'s own field-set section already took for its column list.

**The attribution to Geoffrey Moore is unverified.** No source this research read firsthand attributes the
formula to him. A Goodreads page of reader highlights for Ries and Trout's *Positioning: The Battle for
Your Mind* exists, but it is about a different book entirely, and it carries the perceptual, non-templated
concept of positioning, not a fill-in-the-blank form: "positioning is not what you do to a product.
Positioning is what you do to the mind of the prospect." [[6]](#ref-6) A prior sweep found a Goodreads
reader note on Moore's own *Crossing the Chasm*, but it was not verified against the book itself, and no
chapter title or number was found anywhere. This companion names Moore only as an unverified attribution,
at that strength and no stronger.

**April Dunford's own components and process changed across six years of her own writing.** Her 2020 and
2021 posts list the same five components, in order: competitive alternatives, differentiated features or
capabilities, value for customers, target customer segmentation, and market category
[[7]](#ref-7)[[10]](#ref-10). Her labels drift by year: in 2019 the same five were "competitive
alternatives, unique capabilities, differentiated value, target customers, market category"
[[17]](#ref-17). Her process exists in three distinct versions. A 2019 blog exercise ran five steps, from
"Let go of where you came from" to "Choose your best market frame of reference", and carried no
competitive-alternatives step at all [[8]](#ref-8). The first edition of her book had the same five
components spread across ten steps [[23]](#ref-23). The second edition, which shipped in February 2026,
restructures those ten steps into four prep decisions, five core steps that match the five components, and
a post-work section [[23]](#ref-23)[[31]](#ref-31)[[32]](#ref-32). Any claim about "the book" needs its
edition named; the book itself was not read for this research, and nothing here quotes it
[[33]](#ref-33).

**Her critique of the fill-in-the-blank statement runs across five posts, and it is narrower than it
sounds.** 2017: "The positioning statement: candidate for most useless business tool ever"
[[13]](#ref-13). 2019: a section headed "The Positioning Statement is Harmful and You Should Never Do One",
whose three reasons are that it assumes the answer is already known, that its output cannot be easily
shared or remembered, and that it creates a false sense of being done [[8]](#ref-8). 2020: "the best way I
can describe it is as a sort of "Mad Libs" fill in the blanks exercise for marketing folks."
[[7]](#ref-7) But the same 2019 post that says never do one also says, "Once you have all the pieces
together, you will want to write it down." Her two reasons: a shared record of the agreed positioning, and
a dated record of when it was last reviewed [[8]](#ref-8). **This library's reading, not Dunford's own
words, is that her critique forbids filling the blanks from assumption, and forbids treating the sentence
as the end of the work; it does not forbid a short, dated, internal record of positioning reached by
analysis.** This bundle exists to be exactly that record.

## 3. Anatomy (section by section)

The sections below follow the single-size template in order. There is no lean/full split for this type;
every section ships.

### Purpose and Boundary

**What it is.** A short statement of what this document states, the present-tense competitive stance for a
named target, and what it deliberately rules out by name: messaging, a battlecard, a positioning map, and
`product-vision`'s own future-tense content.

**Why it exists.** Every source that draws a line between positioning and its neighbours draws it the same
direction. Positioning precedes and feeds messaging, never the reverse: "Once you have your positioning
figured out, this becomes a set of inputs to your messaging." [[8]](#ref-8) A battlecard is downstream and
reinforcing, not an input [[48]](#ref-48). A positioning map, a two-axis competitive chart, is a different
artifact entirely, out of this library's templating scope on form grounds, not a content overlap. And this
document is customer positioning specifically: "The methodology in Obviously Awesome is designed
specifically for customer positioning-not investor positioning, not employee positioning, not partner
positioning." [[23]](#ref-23)

**Beginner note.** Name the target customer and the persona the statement is written for before writing
anything else; Dunford calls this a prep decision, not an afterthought [[23]](#ref-23).

**Expert note.** The vision boundary rests on fewer sources than the messaging boundary. Sharebird's two
named practitioners draw the present/future, narrow/broad-audience line directly
[[43]](#ref-43), and Dunford draws the same line from the strategy side [[11]](#ref-11)[[26]](#ref-26). But
a Rational Unified Process vision template nests a "Product Position Statement" subsection inside its own
Vision document [[44]](#ref-44); see Debates, below, before treating the boundary as universal practice.

### The Statement

**What it is.** The fill-in-the-blank sentence itself, the one-line summary of the six components below it.

**Why it exists.** Every admitting source ships some version of this sentence, and three of the five put
the reason to believe after the word `because`, while one gives the differentiation its own closing clause
[[1]](#ref-1)[[2]](#ref-2)[[3]](#ref-3)[[4]](#ref-4). **The field labels in this template are this
library's own wording, not any source's**, because HubSpot's and Yesware's exact bracketed phrasing is
their own copyrighted formula text, quotable here as theirs but never reused as though it were the
template's own.

**Beginner note.** Write this sentence last, after the six components below it are settled, not first.
Dunford's critique of the bare sentence is a critique of writing it from assumption; the sentence as a
*summary* of finished analysis is the use she herself endorses [[7]](#ref-7)[[8]](#ref-8). "These are, in
essence, the blanks of the positioning statement." [[7]](#ref-7)

**Expert note.** The named-alternative slot appears in only two of the five published forms
[[1]](#ref-1)[[9]](#ref-9); its stronger support is not the formula at all, but Dunford's own account of
what positioning describes: "It describes why a specific type of customer should choose your product over
any other alternative way of solving the problem" [[11]](#ref-11).

### The Components

**What it is.** Each named component expanded to a sentence or two: the target, the need, the category,
the benefit, the named alternative, and the differentiation.

**Why it exists.** Dunford's critique of the bare sentence is precisely that it skips the analysis these
components carry: "The only valuable thing we can learn from the statement is that there are multiple
components of positioning." [[7]](#ref-7) MIT's six-field template and the classic form's five-field
template both confirm the same shape under different labels
[[1]](#ref-1)[[9]](#ref-9).

**Beginner note.** List the named alternative first, not last. Dunford's own dependency argument is
explicit: "I had the realization that the starting point had to be competitive alternatives." [[10]](#ref-10)
Her working question for that component is "What would a customer do if your offering didn't exist?"
[[18]](#ref-18), and her answer puts the status quo first: "the first competitive alternative we need to
beat is the status quo." [[18]](#ref-18)

**Expert note.** A phantom competitor, one the team has never actually lost a deal to, does not belong
here: "should not be considered when working on your positioning." [[18]](#ref-18) Adjust only once such a
competitor starts showing up in real deals [[18]](#ref-18)[[56]](#ref-56). Any reference to Dunford's own
component names should quote one of her own lists and give its year; her labels drift, and a secondary
summary's labels are the summary author's, not hers [[9]](#ref-9).

### Why This Positioning

**What it is.** The evidence behind the differentiation claim: what the named alternative actually does,
and why this target should believe the stated benefit over it.

**Why it exists.** HubSpot's formula closes on exactly this question, "reason why target market should
believe your differentiation statement" [[2]](#ref-2), and this library's reading of why Dunford wants the
record written down at all is that an unrecorded reason is a reason nobody outside the room can check
[[8]](#ref-8)[[25]](#ref-25).

**Beginner note.** State the proof, not just the claim. "The "reasons to believe" are any proof points or
evidence that show your customers how you live up to your claims about how you are different and better."
[[59]](#ref-59)

**Expert note.** Two practices are well sourced but not shown inside any document this research found:
recording how the positioning was tested with customers before it was adopted
[[8]](#ref-8)[[10]](#ref-10)[[55]](#ref-55)[[56]](#ref-56), and recording which other framing the team
considered and rejected, since "every product can be positioned in multiple different ways"
[[8]](#ref-8). Neither is a sourced requirement of the document itself; treat both as this library's own
reasoning about what a record of reasoning should include, not a cited convention.

### When We Revisit It

**What it is.** The date of the last review and the named events that call for an early one, not a
calendar entry alone.

**Why it exists.** Dunford's own reason for writing positioning down at all includes this: "The document is
dated so you can track when the positioning was last reviewed." [[8]](#ref-8) Separately, she names both
positive triggers, an acquisition, a funding round, a senior hire, and negative ones, a bad quarter, lost
deals, an outage, an unplanned leadership change [[22]](#ref-22). CLIMB adds market and competitive shifts
and a mismatch the team discovers through customer research [[51]](#ref-51).

**Beginner note.** A periodic check-in is sourced as practice, not as a single standard: Dunford describes
a roughly six-month cadence at one company [[26]](#ref-26) and "a couple of times a year" elsewhere
[[30]](#ref-30); Lawson Abinanti recommends auditing every twelve months [[52]](#ref-52). Dunford frames a
check-in's own central question as "does any of that shift our value themes?" and says "Frequently the
answer is no." [[27]](#ref-27)

**Expert note.** The sources genuinely split on calendar against trigger, and this split is the open
question behind this type's own `foundation`/`utility` classification; see Debates, below. Nothing in this
section may say that no source states a cadence. Three do, and a fourth asks for a dated record precisely
so the cadence can be checked [[8]](#ref-8)[[26]](#ref-26)[[30]](#ref-30)[[52]](#ref-52).

## 4. Variants and sizing

**One size ships: `lean`.** Every admitting source agrees the document is short, and none describes a
longer, terminal version of the statement itself. MIT: "A product positioning statement forces you to use
a one page format to summarize everything that matters for your product." [[1]](#ref-1) HubSpot: "Aim for
no more than three to five sentences, if possible." [[2]](#ref-2) Yesware: "Positioning statements should
be relatively short (3 - 5 sentences, at the most)." [[4]](#ref-4) Product Marketing Alliance's own
field-by-field template could not be read past a membership gate, so its length is not confirmed here
[[5]](#ref-5). **Dunford's own material is genuinely longer**, a
12-page workbook given to her newsletter subscribers [[12]](#ref-12), but this research reads it as a
facilitation process for arriving at the one-page statement, not a second, heavier version of the statement
document itself. A `full` variant that tried to carry her workbook's process inside the template would
misplace facilitation content as document structure; that material belongs in the guide.

## 5. Methodology lineage

**No source read ties the positioning statement by name to Scrum, SAFe, Shape Up, Amazon's
working-backwards process, Lean, or design thinking.** That is this research's own absence, not a claim
that any of those schools reject it; nothing here should be read as evidence either way.

**The one named software-development methodology this research found assigns positioning a place, and
merges it into a different document.** The Rational Unified Process nests a "Product Position Statement"
subsection directly inside its own Vision document template: "Provide an overall statement summarizing at
the highest level, the unique position the product intends to fill in the marketplace." [[44]](#ref-44)
This is a general software-engineering artifact, not a product-management or product-marketing source, and
it is the research's one data point against treating vision and positioning as always separate; see
Debates, below.

**This library's own reading is that Dunford's body of work is the closest thing the field has produced to
a named positioning methodology**, and it is growth and product-marketing practice, not a delivery or
planning method. Her components depend on each
other in a fixed order, starting from competitive alternatives [[10]](#ref-10), and her process has gone
through three versions across six years without ever becoming a named framework the way the North Star
Framework did for growth metrics [[8]](#ref-8)[[23]](#ref-23)[[31]](#ref-31).

**One camp rejects the positioning frame itself, rather than tailoring it.** Christopher Lochhead's
Category Design argues that positioning, by definition, accepts an existing market's frame rather than
building a new one: "Because positioning-by definition-is about placing your company inside an existing
market. It assumes the aisle already exists." [[58]](#ref-58) "Positioning asks: "Where do we fit?"
Category Design asks: "What kind of future are we building?"" [[58]](#ref-58) This bundle does not adopt
that camp's conclusion, since this library has already chosen to build a positioning document, but it
names the camp here because methodology lineage includes those who reject or replace the artifact, not only
those who refine it.

## 6. Debates and contested boundaries

**Where it sits in the chain, against the vision and the strategy.** This library places positioning third
in the `strategy-docs` chain, between the product strategy and the North Star metric, and the family
contract names that placement a POSITION rather than a finding
([`strategy-docs.md`](../../docs/internal/contracts/strategy-docs.md)). No source states this exact
sequence. The strongest support for an order at all is Dunford's own account that positioning follows the
strategy rather than preceding it: "I don't see it as quite that simple, particularly because our
positioning will change with each step of our strategy." [[11]](#ref-11) "Positioning changes over time as
we follow our strategy on the journey to the vision. At each step, you will have different positioning."
[[11]](#ref-11) **The considered alternative is second, between the vision and the strategy.** Devika Nair,
Director of Product Management at Oracle, frames positioning "as part of the vision and decision making"
[[39]](#ref-39), which argues for a place closer to the vision rather than after the strategy. This
companion states both because the choice is argued, not sourced, and a reader who treats third place as
settled is overstating what the research found.

**Who owns the statement.** Two sources name product marketing by title: Blackblot, "Product positioning
statements are created by the product marketer who belongs to the Product Marketing department"
[[34]](#ref-34), and Young Urban Project, "product positioning is usually owned by the Product Marketing
function" [[35]](#ref-35). A third, Douglas Karr's account of the Pragmatic Framework, places positioning
inside product marketing's own planning work [[42]](#ref-42). Four sources describe joint or split work
instead: Aha!'s guide calls it "a cross-functional exercise" involving product management and product
marketing "working closely together" [[36]](#ref-36), and its blog separately describes it as "a strategic
exercise typically carried out by product management and product marketing teams," where "close
collaboration is important" [[38]](#ref-38). Apart from either, Aha! files a product-positioning template
in its own product-strategy guide, separate from a marketing-positioning template elsewhere on the same
page [[37]](#ref-37), a catalog placement rather than a description of joint work. Nair splits the work by
task: "Product Marketing is responsibile for how we position these products to the customers." Her next
sentence keeps product management in the room: "While product management is also responsible for the
positioning, they are looking at it as part of the vision and decision making." [[39]](#ref-39) A third shape names neither function alone: the CEO or business owner
drives the effort in Dunford's account, with product, marketing and sales building it together
[[30]](#ref-30)[[53]](#ref-53). **No source states this library's own default**, that product management
owns the statement as product direction while product marketing drafts or co-writes it. Where the sources
lean, they lean toward product marketing [[34]](#ref-34)[[35]](#ref-35)[[42]](#ref-42). This companion's
guide carries that default labelled as the library's own reasoning, never as a sourced finding.

**Whether the document is revisited on a calendar or on a trigger.** Dunford gives both inside a single
post, a roughly six-month check-in plus emergency check-ins for big events [[26]](#ref-26). Abinanti names
a flat twelve months [[52]](#ref-52). CLIMB names only triggers, and warns that quarterly rewriting is
usually a sign the strategy underneath was never firmly decided [[51]](#ref-51). The Starr Conspiracy calls
positioning itself "stable for years," though its own cadence and trigger language is scoped to a messaging
framework, not to the positioning statement [[46]](#ref-46). **This library's own test for the type's
`foundation` classification, argued-and-durable against maintained-and-periodic, does not resolve cleanly
here**, and ADR 0076 (positioning-statement joins strategy-docs as a sixth member) recommends `foundation`
and names `utility` as the considered alternative; the choice is the maintainer's, not something the
sources settle
([ADR 0076](../../docs/internal/decisions/0076-positioning-statement-joins-strategy-docs-as-a-sixth-member.md)).

**Whether positioning and vision are one document or two.** Two documents, in Sharebird's and Dunford's
accounts [[43]](#ref-43)[[11]](#ref-11)[[26]](#ref-26). One document with a positioning subsection, in the
Rational Unified Process [[44]](#ref-44). This companion follows the two-document reading, since it is the
stronger and more recent evidence, and names the RUP counter-example rather than hiding it.

**Whether the fill-in-the-blank statement should exist at all.** Dunford's own answer is "You Should Never
Do One" [[8]](#ref-8), immediately followed by her own instruction to write positioning down once it is
reached by analysis [[8]](#ref-8). Heinz's secondary summary inverts her stance by framing her five
components as building "a strong positioning statement" [[9]](#ref-9), which this research reads as the
summary author's own framing, not hers. Lochhead rejects positioning itself, not just the statement, in
favour of Category Design [[58]](#ref-58); see Methodology lineage, above.

**Whether a sixth component, trends, belongs.** Heinz's summary lists "Relevant Trends (Bonus)"
[[9]](#ref-9). Koji calls timing and trends optional: "Relevant Trends (optional) - the "why now" context."
[[56]](#ref-56) Dunford's own lists, in every year this research read, carry five components, never six
[[7]](#ref-7)[[10]](#ref-10).
Only her own book could settle whether trends are canon, and the book was not read [[33]](#ref-33).

## 7. Anti-patterns and failure modes

- **Filling the sentence in from assumption instead of from the six components' own analysis.** This is
  Dunford's central, five-post critique, not a minor stylistic complaint
  [[7]](#ref-7)[[8]](#ref-8)[[13]](#ref-13).
- **Positioning against a phantom competitor**, one the team has never actually lost a real deal to.
  "should not be considered when working on your positioning." [[18]](#ref-18)
- **Treating the document as messaging, or importing messaging's cadence and triggers into it.** "Lock the
  positioning statement first." [[46]](#ref-46) The Starr Conspiracy's own quarterly review cadence and
  trigger list govern a messaging framework, not this document [[46]](#ref-46).
- **Writing it once and never referring to it again.** "Like other totally useless business exercises - it
  is completed and never, ever referred to again." [[8]](#ref-8)
- **Rewriting it every quarter**, which usually signals an unfinished strategy rather than a positioning
  problem: "the problem likely isn't the statement. It's that the underlying strategic choices haven't been
  made firmly enough." [[51]](#ref-51)
- **A differentiation claim that fits any competitor's name as easily as this product's.** The desk test:
  "If you can substitute any competitor's name for your own in the positioning statement-and it still
  sounds credible-then you need some additional work on your differentiators and competitive advantages."
  [[59]](#ref-59)
- **Crediting the formula to Geoffrey Moore**, or citing a Ries-and-Trout source for his book. See Origins,
  above.

## 9. Adaptations

**B2B against consumer.** Positioning shifts are expected and normal for B2B products, and rare for
consumer ones, in Dunford's own account: "Consumer products rarely shift positioning, where positioning
shifts are expected and normal for B2B products." [[26]](#ref-26)

**Company size decides who leads the work, not who owns the document.** "For smaller companies, the CEO
needs to drive the effort (for bigger companies, it should be whoever is ultimately responsible for the
business the product is part of)." [[30]](#ref-30)

**Acquisitions.** Both sides of an acquisition get a genuine occasion to revisit positioning together: "An
acquisition - for both the acquiring company and the company that has been acquired - is a golden
opportunity to revisit and tighten up positioning." [[28]](#ref-28) A new product that adds value the
parent product did not previously deliver changes the parent's own positioning the same way a major
release would [[28]](#ref-28).

**More than one statement at once.** Blackblot names this directly as situational rather than a rule: "It
all depends on the given situation. It is possible that separate product positioning statements will be
needed or perhaps only one product positioning statement will suffice." [[34]](#ref-34)
