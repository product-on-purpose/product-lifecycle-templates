LEAN POSITIONING STATEMENT. This bundle ships one size, deliberately. See
positioning-statement_guide.md, "Picking a variant," for why; that reasoning is not repeated
here.

WHAT THIS DOCUMENT IS, AND IS NOT. A positioning statement is a short, internal, present-tense
record of how this product stands against a named alternative, for a specific target customer,
now. It is reached by analysis, not filled in from assumption: April Dunford's own five-post
critique of the bare fill-in-the-blank sentence is a critique of writing it from assumption and
treating it as finished work, not a ban on writing positioning down once the analysis behind it is
settled. "Once you have all the pieces together, you will want to write it down," she says, for
two reasons: a shared record of what the team agreed, and a dated record of when it was last
reviewed. This document is that record. It is NOT product-vision (that is long-term, aspirational
and future-facing; this is present-day and competitive). It is NOT messaging (the external
language built on top of this positioning, after it is settled; this library has not yet admitted
a messaging-framework bundle). It is NOT a battlecard (downstream and reinforcing, not an input).
It is NOT a positioning map (a two-axis competitive chart, a different artifact, out of this
library's scope on form grounds). See positioning-statement_companion.md section 1 and section 8
for the fuller set of boundaries, and section 2 before crediting this formula to Geoffrey Moore: no
source this research read firsthand attributes it to him, and this template names him only as an
unverified attribution, never as a sourced fact.

WHO FILLS THIS IN. No source read states this library's own default, that product management holds
the statement as product direction while product marketing drafts or co-writes it. Where the
sources lean, three name product marketing as the owner. This template's owner field
carries whichever answer your own organization gives; do not read a single correct owner into it.
See positioning-statement_companion.md section 6 (Who owns the statement) before filling in the
owner field.

WHERE THIS SITS IN THE CHAIN. This library places positioning third among its strategy-docs
family, between the product strategy and the North Star metric, and names that placement its own
POSITION rather than a sourced finding. A considered alternative places it second, between the
vision and the strategy, on one practitioner's framing of positioning as part of the vision and
decision making. Nothing in this template depends on which place is correct; it depends only on
positioning following a strategy that has already named a target segment. See
positioning-statement_companion.md section 6 (Debates).

2. Replace each {{placeholder}} with your content. Write The Statement last, after The Components

3. If a section does not apply, write "N/A" and one line of why, rather than deleting it.
4. This is a standing record, revisited on the schedule and the triggers named in When We Revisit

     GOOD  "This document states how Orbital stands against the paper schedule and the group

           and not a battlecard."
           (names the alternative, the target, the audience, and the three things it is not)
     WEAK  "This describes what makes Orbital special."
           (no named alternative, no named target, no stated audience, no boundary)

{{product_name}}'s positioning statement states how this product stands against
{{named_alternative_short}}, for {{target_customer_short_name}}, as of {{date}}. It is
internal, written for the people who make product, marketing and sales decisions about
{{product_name}}, not for customers or the press.

This document deliberately does not state:

- **Messaging**: the external language and talking points a team builds on top of this
  positioning, once it is settled.
- **A battlecard**: sales-facing competitive responses, downstream of and reinforcing this
  positioning, never an input to it.
- **A positioning map**: a two-axis competitive chart, a different artifact.
- **{{product_name}}'s product vision**: the long-term, aspirational, future-facing destination
  this product is working toward. This document is present-day and competitive, not aspirational.

## The Statement

<!-- WHAT  The fill-in-the-blank sentence itself, the one-line summary of the six components
           below it.
     WHY   Every admitting source ships some version of this sentence: MIT, HubSpot, eCornell and
           Yesware each publish a slot-based formula, no two identical. The field labels below are
           this library's own wording, not any one source's exact phrasing, since HubSpot's and

     GOOD  "For shift-work managers at single-site retail and food-service businesses who need

           (every clause traces to a component below it, and the reason to believe sits right
           after "because")
     WEAK  "For businesses that need to schedule shifts, Orbital is scheduling software that

           analysis that lives in The Components below, not a substitute for that analysis. -->

## The Components

<!-- WHAT  Each named component expanded to a sentence or two: the named alternative, the
           target, the need, the category, the benefit, and the differentiation. Listed in this
           order, alternative first, because the rest of the analysis depends on it.
     WHY   Dunford's own dependency argument is explicit: "I had the realization that the
           starting point had to be competitive alternatives," because "your differentiated
           features are only 'differentiated' when you compare them to competitive
           alternatives." Her working question for that first component is "What would a
           customer do if your offering didn't exist?" and her own answer puts the status quo
           first: "the first competitive alternative we need to beat is the status quo." MIT's
           six-field template and the classic form's five-field template both confirm
           the same shape under different labels. Deep dive:
           positioning-statement_companion.md section 3 (The Components).
     ASK   What would this target customer do today if {{product_name}} did not exist? Is the
           named alternative a competitor the team has actually lost real deals to, or a phantom
           competitor nobody has really lost to yet? Who specifically is the target, by what
           they need rather than by a demographic label? What market category gives this target
           the right frame of reference? What benefit does this target get that the alternative
           does not? What makes that benefit genuinely different, not just differently worded?
     GOOD  "Named alternative: the paper schedule taped to the stockroom wall, plus the group
           chat managers use to cover last-minute call-outs. It is free, already in place, and
           good enough for most weeks."
           (names a real, specific status quo rather than a vague competitor)
     WEAK  "Named alternative: other scheduling software."
           (no specific rival named, and the status quo, the far more common real rival, is
           skipped)
     TRAP  Naming a competitor the team has never actually lost a real deal to. Dunford is
           direct about this: a phantom competitor "should not be considered when working on
           your positioning." Adjust only once such a competitor starts showing up in real
           deals. -->
