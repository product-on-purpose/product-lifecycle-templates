# Project Brief: the companion

The reference behind the template. Read this when you want to know why a section exists, what the
research actually says, and where two senses of "project brief," and two schools of practice, disagree
with each other.

## 1. Orientation

A project brief turns a mandate into a request for authority to start. The UK government's own guide puts
it in one sentence: "In essence, the Project Brief says why the project is needed, what it must achieve and
who should be involved" [[6]](#ref-6). It is written before commitment, in AXELOS's own phrase, created
"pre-project during the Starting up a Project process" [[1]](#ref-1), for whoever owns the decision:
"primarily written for the project board" [[2]](#ref-2). Its approval authorizes further planning, not
delivery, and once initiation documentation exists it stops being consulted: "the project brief is no
longer used in project management activities" [[2]](#ref-2).

At a glance:

- **The name covers two different documents, and this bundle builds one of them.** The lightweight sense is
  short, written before commitment, and superseded once initiation documentation exists
  [[1]](#ref-1)[[2]](#ref-2)[[31]](#ref-31). A second, heavyweight sense is large, formally gated and
  revised across a project's life [[8]](#ref-8)[[9]](#ref-9)[[32]](#ref-32). A reader who came looking for
  the heavyweight kind is told plainly, in section 8 below, that it exists and is not this bundle.
- **No composition is standard.** The standards body's own glossary names four elements; a practitioner
  mirror names seven; the UK government's own checklist runs to fourteen. Published templates range from
  four "key elements" to ten numbered sections. See section 3 and the Debates below, and note which source
  is actually being credited each time: prince2.wiki's own synthesis is not the same claim as "PRINCE2
  says."
- **Whether approval marks the start of the project is genuinely contested**, and that contest is the
  strain behind this bundle's own family placement. See section 6.
- **No source measured whether writing a brief changes a project's outcome.** Several sources recommend an
  explicit exclusions list; none of them measured whether having one reduces scope creep, and this companion
  does not repeat that claim as fact. See section 7.

## 2. Origins and evolution

The standards-body source for the term is AXELOS's PRINCE2 glossary, read via the Internet Archive because
the file's live host, `axelos.com`, no longer serves it. The copy carries version "v.1.1" and a 2012
copyright notice; only its file name marks it as the PRINCE2 2009 edition glossary, and this companion
states that attribution as resting on the file name, not on a date printed in the document itself
[[1]](#ref-1). Its one-paragraph definition names "the purpose, cost, time and performance requirements, and
constraints for a project," created "pre-project," and "superseded by the Project Initiation Documentation
and not maintained" [[1]](#ref-1).

prince2.wiki, a practitioner site publishing structured summaries of PRINCE2 process and management-product
pages under a stated Creative Commons Attribution licence [[2]](#ref-2), is this research's main source for
how the brief is actually described in current practitioner writing: "a short, high-level overview of the
project, created during the starting up a project process by the project manager" [[2]](#ref-2). Because the
site uses AXELOS's trade marks under a permission of unstated scope, this companion describes its structure
in its own words rather than adapting its page text directly.

The UK government's own contribution is a primary source this bundle can actually adapt. The Department for
Business, Innovation and Skills published *Guidelines for Managing Projects* in November 2010 under the Open
Government Licence, which states outright: "You may re-use this information (not including logos) free of
charge in any format or medium, under the terms of the Open Government Licence" [[6]](#ref-6). Its guide is
this bundle's adaptable source for template guidance text, distinct from AXELOS's all-rights-reserved
glossary, which may be quoted briefly but never adapted [[1]](#ref-1).

**The term is still in current UK government use, though not where this research expected to find it.**
Government Functional Standard GovS 002, "Project delivery" (v2.1), was read end to end via a third-party
mirror, and its Annex B glossary carries no entry for "project
brief" despite the standard's own text pointing readers to "the common glossary of definitions" for exactly
that kind of term [[7]](#ref-7). The one passage that does name a brief sits in the body, among the senior
responsible owner's accountabilities: "the vision, justification and desired outcomes for the programme or
project, together with any strategic assumptions, are documented in a programme or project brief"
[[7]](#ref-7). That sentence shows the term in live use; it does not define it, and this companion does not
attribute a definition to GovS 002.

**Not every jurisdiction places the document at the same point in the sequence.** Tasmania's own guidelines
say the project manager "prepares a Project Brief once the project has been approved by the Project
Sponsor and/or Project Steering Committee," to document "what is to occur in the INITIATION Phase" [[10]](#ref-10).
That is after, rather than before, the decision the PRINCE2 and BIS lineage places the brief in front of. This
companion follows AXELOS, prince2.wiki and BIS, and names Tasmania's usage as a named divergence rather than
silently picking a side; see the Debates section.

**What this research could not read.** The PRINCE2 6th (2017) and 7th (2023) edition manuals sit behind a
subscription; the one AXELOS source read here is the 2009-edition glossary. AXELOS's own PRINCE2 Agile
Appendix A.19 product description, which would have settled a live composition question below, is
unreachable: its host no longer resolves and no archived copy exists. The Association for Project
Management's Body of Knowledge, 7th edition, is a members-only publication; the file this research could
read is the one circulated for the 2025 consultation, not the published standard, and a separate sample
extract from the publisher does not carry the sentence the consultation file does [[22]](#ref-22). The PMI
Lexicon of Project Management Terms, version 5.0, was read in full and has no "project brief" entry at all
[[29]](#ref-29).

## 3. Anatomy (section by section)

### Background and Mandate (lean and full)

Where the project comes from, and why it starts now. BIS names the trigger precisely: "Start-up is triggered
when a Senior Responsible Owner (SRO) agrees/decides to take responsibility for a new initiative that might
best be run as a project," coming "from business planning, an external driver (e.g. EU legislation,
compliance requirement) or identification of a significant problem that cannot be dealt with as a matter of
routine" [[6]](#ref-6). The mandate itself is AXELOS's own defined term, "an external product generated by
the authority commissioning the project that forms the trigger for Starting up a Project" [[1]](#ref-1), and
its form varies widely: BIS calls it "often as simple as an email" [[6]](#ref-6), Litten's PRINCE2 7th
edition reading says it "can vary in form from a verbal instruction to a well-defined and justified project
definition" [[31]](#ref-31), and prince2.wiki adds that the brief itself "is based on the project mandate,
which may contain limited or detailed information depending on the source" [[2]](#ref-2). The "why now"
question is BIS's own checklist item, "Background - why does this project need to be done and why now?"
[[6]](#ref-6).

### Objectives (lean and full)

What the project must achieve, stated so a reader can later tell whether it did. BIS's checklist names them
"achievable and measurable (SMART)" [[6]](#ref-6) and elsewhere states the purpose directly: "Project
objectives define what a project must achieve for it to be judged to be complete and successful and hence
able to be closed" [[6]](#ref-6). prince2.wiki repeats the SMART bar as a quality criterion for the brief as
a whole [[2]](#ref-2). **How firmly sources hold this bar varies.** Oxford City Council's own template
qualifies it, "wherever possible in specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, and time-based terms"
[[15]](#ref-15); one practitioner source states the requirement outright, "Avoid vague verbs like improve,
optimise or streamline, because nobody can tell when they are done" [[30]](#ref-30); another vendor template
carries no measurability wording for this field at all [[16]](#ref-16). Treat the SMART bar as the
recommendation most sources converge on, not as a universal requirement every published template enforces.

### Scope and Exclusions (lean and full)

What is in, and, as its own named field rather than folded into a general paragraph, what is explicitly out.
BIS's checklist item is exactly that pairing: "Scope - what in and what's out" [[6]](#ref-6). Oxford City
Council gives the exclusion half its own numbered heading, "3 Project Scope and Exclusions," with the
prompt "What is outside the remit of the project?" [[15]](#ref-15), and a vendor template gives it a section
of its own too, "Section 4: Out of Scope" [[12]](#ref-12). **Deliverables belong here, as what is in scope**:
BIS's own contents list names "Deliverables" and its checklist "Deliverables/outcomes" [[6]](#ref-6), Oxford
City Council carries "10.1 Project Deliverables" [[15]](#ref-15), and Maldon District Council's template
asks for "Expected outcomes and key deliverables" [[16]](#ref-16). Several sources recommend the exclusions
field specifically because an unwritten boundary invites later disputes over what was agreed; **none of them
measured whether writing one actually reduces scope creep**, and this companion states the recommendation,
not a measured effect. See section 7.

### Outline Business Justification (lean and full)

Why the project is worth doing, in outline only, with a full comparison of options deferred to a named later
approval. prince2.wiki names "outline business case" as one item in its own composition list for the brief
[[2]](#ref-2), and prince2.ca's comparison of the brief against PMBOK's charter states plainly, "the main
difference between the charter and brief, is that in PRINCE2, the creation of the business case (in outline
form) is part of the project brief" [[18]](#ref-18). Oxford City Council gives it a numbered section of its
own, "10 Outline Business Case" [[15]](#ref-15). BIS's own contents list carries "The reasons for the
project," "Desired benefits and who will realise them," and "Outline estimates of time and cost"
[[6]](#ref-6); a vendor template asks separately for the "Value of performing this proposed project" and the
"Impact of not performing this proposed project" [[33]](#ref-33). **This section's boundary against the
library's own `business-case` bundle is load-bearing and already stated on the other side of it**: that
guide names the choice directly, "a project brief absorbs an outline business case as one component and
retires once initiation documentation exists; the business case itself keeps being refined, it does not
retire with the brief" (`business-case_guide.md:17`). This section states that a fuller comparison of
options, including doing nothing, is deferred to that document; it does not attempt the comparison itself.
It also carries the outline estimate of time and cost that several sources name as part of the brief itself
[[1]](#ref-1)[[6]](#ref-6)[[33]](#ref-33), sized to one or two lines, not a cost breakdown: one vendor's
contrary four-element brief drops the budget figure entirely [[14]](#ref-14), so this is stated as this
library's own sizing choice, not a point every source agrees on.

### Constraints and Who Should Be Involved (lean and full)

Known constraints on the work, and the people who need to be part of it. BIS states the pairing directly in
its summary sentence, "the Project Brief says why the project is needed, what it must achieve and who should
be involved" [[6]](#ref-6), and separately lists "Constraints that must be taken into consideration during
the project," "Stakeholders (internal and external)," and "Dependencies on other
projects/activities/decisions" among the brief's contents [[6]](#ref-6). AXELOS's own glossary names
"constraints" as part of the standards-body definition [[1]](#ref-1). **One phrase this research
corrected**: an earlier reading attributed "project management team structure" to prince2.wiki's project
brief page itself; raw-checked, that phrase is absent from that page and verbatim instead on prince2.wiki's
separate starting-up-a-project page, describing what the wider process produces and approves, not a named
field of the brief document [[3]](#ref-3). This companion attributes the phrase to that page, not to the
brief's own composition list. **Known risks and assumptions belong here too, as a line or two each, not a
formal register**: prince2.wiki's own composition list names "risks" [[2]](#ref-2), its starting-up page
describes the brief as covering "the scope, objectives, risks, and approach" [[3]](#ref-3), BIS lists
"Assumptions" and "Risks" among the brief's contents and "Any known risks" in its checklist [[6]](#ref-6),
and a vendor template names "Project influences and dependencies" [[33]](#ref-33). A formal risk-scoring
table stays out of this section at any size; that belongs to the heavyweight sense named in section 8.

### Decision Requested (lean and full)

What approval is being asked for, from whom, and what happens next. prince2.wiki states the decision
plainly: "It is used by the project board to make the first key decision: whether to authorise the
initiation stage" [[2]](#ref-2), and its companion page on starting up a project names the request itself as
that process's final output, "to send a request to the project board to initiate the project, which is to
allow the project's first stage to start" [[3]](#ref-3). BIS lists what the approvers are confirming when
they sign off, ending with a commitment to plan rather than to build: they agree to give the project
manager time and resources, "needed to plan the project in detail and to produce the Project Initiation
Document (PID)" [[6]](#ref-6). One practitioner source frames the moment of approval itself as the
thing that changes the document's status: "A brief that is filled in but never approved is a wish list, not
a mandate; the signature is what turns it into permission to start" [[30]](#ref-30), and another puts the
same instruction more bluntly, "Get it approved by the decider, out loud" [[11]](#ref-11). Oxford City
Council's template carries its own approvals and authority sections [[15]](#ref-15), and a vendor template
has a dedicated approval block, "PROJECT BRIEF DOCUMENT APPROVAL" [[33]](#ref-33). **This section also
carries the family contract's own obligation**: every `discovery-docs` member must say, in its companion,
what document takes over once the decision is made. Naming that document here, and recording the approver's
name and the answer, is this library's own structural choice, not a claim that a named external source
requires this exact field.

### Project Approach (full only)

How the work will be approached: build, buy, or a mix. prince2.wiki names this as an output of the same
process that assembles the brief, "Select the project approach and assemble the project brief"
[[3]](#ref-3), and Oxford City Council's numbered template gives it a section of its own, "7 Project
Approach" [[15]](#ref-15). A vendor template asks for a "Proposed delivery approach" and offers its own
named options, "Package/Custom/Enhancement/Outsourced" [[33]](#ref-33); that specific vocabulary is one
vendor's own, attributed here to Bestoutcome, not offered as PRINCE2's own categorisation. No source read
for this bundle elaborates the approach section beyond naming that it exists, so the guidance behind this
section describes the choice in general terms, build, buy or a mix, rather than adopting one vendor's
category set as a standard.

### Relationship to Other Documents (full only)

Where this document sits against the mandate that triggers it, the business case it absorbs in outline, and
the initiation documentation that supersedes it. Supersession is stated consistently across the standards
and practitioner lineage: AXELOS's glossary itself, "superseded by the Project Initiation Documentation and
not maintained" [[1]](#ref-1); prince2.wiki, "Once the project initiation documentation (PID) is created, the
project brief is no longer used in project management activities" [[2]](#ref-2); the PID's own prince2.wiki
page, "The project brief is developed during the starting up a project process" and folded into the PID at
the end of initiation [[4]](#ref-4); and Litten's reading of the current edition, "The Project Brief is not
used after the Initiation stage" [[31]](#ref-31). One practitioner source frames the PID plainly for a reader
who has not met PRINCE2 vocabulary before: "A PID (Project Initiation Document) is the detailed plan - scope,
schedule, governance, roles and risk - that is maintained as the project runs," adding, "many small projects
never need a PID at all" [[30]](#ref-30). Neither a project charter nor a PID is built in this library, and
this section says so plainly rather than leaving a reader to discover the gap by searching for a template
that does not exist; see section 8 for the charter boundary itself, and section 8 again for the product,
creative and design briefs, and the Project Canvas, that a reader might otherwise confuse this document with.

## 4. Variants and sizing

**Lean** carries Background and Mandate, Objectives, Scope and Exclusions, Outline Business Justification,
Constraints and Who Should Be Involved, and Decision Requested: enough to ask for authority to start without
naming how the work will be approached or mapping it against neighbouring documents. **Full** inserts
Project Approach and Relationship to Other Documents. Unlike this library's `business-case` bundle, where the
full variant adds real financial weight, this bundle's full variant stays short: two sources that speak to
overall length converge on staying brief regardless of size, prince2.wiki's own quality bar, "short, focused"
[[2]](#ref-2), and Smartsheet's stronger claim that the whole document "should be a single page long, and
anyone should be able to understand it at a glance" [[19]](#ref-19). **Length guidance is otherwise genuinely
inconsistent across sources**: Storyflow reports "often one to two pages" [[11]](#ref-11), Lark reports "1 to
2 pages" and warns that "highly complex projects might stretch slightly longer but should rarely exceed three
pages" [[13]](#ref-13), Asana states a brief "might be as short as a paragraph" or "might run up to a page,"
and separately, in its FAQ, "A project brief should be one page or less" [[14]](#ref-14). BIS, the primary
source, states no fixed length at all, scaling instead to the project: "For small projects this will be a
very short document often with only a few sentences for each section; larger projects may require more
detail" [[6]](#ref-6). No source measured length against outcome; treat every figure above as one author's
convention, not a rule.

**Whether a project this small needs a brief at all is a live disagreement, not a settled rule.** BIS itself
allows a short, well-defined, certain project to skip straight to the PID "without the intermediate step of
the Project Brief" [[6]](#ref-6). One practitioner source agrees from the other direction, "many small
projects never need a PID at all" [[30]](#ref-30), and Lauren Selley's trilogy piece frames the choice as a
matter of scale, "Smaller projects may suffice with a Project Brief, while larger ones might need a PID"
[[20]](#ref-20). Tasmania's guidelines go further and let the brief stand in for a different document
entirely on small work, "the Project Brief is owned by the Project Sponsor and may be used instead of a
Project Business Plan for small projects" [[10]](#ref-10). These sources are not all describing the same
choice: BIS is deciding whether to skip the brief, the others whether to skip the document that follows it.
State both positions and let the reader's own project size decide, rather than resolving the disagreement
here.

## 5. Methodology lineage

The one methodology this research confirmed by an official-tier source is PRINCE2, through AXELOS's own
glossary [[1]](#ref-1). prince2.wiki's practitioner mirror is the fuller composition picture this bundle
leans on for structure, published under a stated Creative Commons Attribution licence but using AXELOS's
trade marks under a permission of unstated scope, which is why this companion describes its structure in its
own words [[2]](#ref-2)[[3]](#ref-3). The UK government's own guide is the one source this bundle can adapt
outright, under the Open Government Licence [[6]](#ref-6), and Government Functional Standard GovS 002 shows
the term still circulating in current UK government usage without itself supplying a definition
[[7]](#ref-7). Knowledge Train's own reading of the PRINCE2 management-product set corroborates the short
summary and the supersession relationship independently of prince2.wiki [[5]](#ref-5).

A separate, heavier tradition publishes something also called a project brief that is large, iterative and
gate-adjacent, not a shorter cousin of the PRINCE2 sense. NSW Health mandates a template "for the development
of project briefs for projects valued at less than $10 million," structured "in four Parts" whose completion
requirement scales with capital value [[8]](#ref-8). The Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat's guide
describes "an iterative document that progressively reflects the state of the project with each update"
[[9]](#ref-9), resubmitted "at regular and meaningful intervals throughout the life-cycle of the project"
[[9]](#ref-9). Ireland's National Transport Authority distils a "Preliminary Project Brief" at its first
gateway and develops it into "a Detailed Project Brief, which is a deliverable required within Phase 5,"
kept under change control the whole time, "any proposed changes in subsequent Phases to the Project Brief
shall only be implemented with the approval of the NTA" [[32]](#ref-32). Maldon District Council's own
committee template sits between the two senses: it is titled a "Project Report Template (Detailed brief),"
carries a SWOT block and funding rows, and shows no exclusions field and no measurability wording of its own
[[16]](#ref-16).

A third, practitioner and vendor family publishes short templates in the PRINCE2/BIS lightweight sense but
outside its vocabulary entirely, and disagrees with itself on section count and order: Storyflow's ten
sections [[11]](#ref-11), a vendor's seven [[12]](#ref-12), Lark's five "core elements" [[13]](#ref-13),
Asana's four "key elements," stated with the explicit caveat that "there's no one-size-fits-all project brief
template or style" [[14]](#ref-14), and Oxford City Council's own ten numbered sections, the only template
this research read that also carries its own Project Options and Outline Business Case headings
[[15]](#ref-15).

**Against all three traditions, PMI is silent.** The PMI Lexicon of Project Management Terms, version 5.0,
defines "project charter" and does not define, or otherwise use, "project brief" anywhere in its text
[[29]](#ref-29). The Association for Project Management's own consultation draft of its Body of Knowledge,
7th edition, treats the gap differently, naming "project brief" as one of several interchangeable labels
rather than a missing concept: "different names for this rationale in common usage include 'problem
statement', 'project brief' and 'project charter'" [[22]](#ref-22). That sentence rests on the consultation
file alone; a separate sample extract from the same publisher does not carry it, so it cannot be treated as
confirmed by two independent copies of the same standard [[22]](#ref-22).

## 6. Debates and contested boundaries

**What does the brief actually contain?** AXELOS's own glossary names four things, "the purpose, cost, time
and performance requirements, and constraints" [[1]](#ref-1). prince2.wiki's composition synthesis names
seven, "background, objectives, scope, constraints, risks, stakeholders, and outline business case"
[[2]](#ref-2). prince2.wiki's own starting-up page adds "roles & responsibilities," "six performance
targets," the approach, and "project management team structure" as things the wider process assembles into
the brief [[3]](#ref-3). BIS's contents list runs to eleven bullets and its start-up checklist to fourteen
items [[6]](#ref-6). Published templates disagree just as widely: ten sections, seven, five, four, ten again
[[11]](#ref-11)[[12]](#ref-12)[[13]](#ref-13)[[14]](#ref-14)[[15]](#ref-15). **No composition is standard**,
and each list above is attributed to its actual source in this companion, never generically to "PRINCE2" or
"AXELOS," because only [[1]](#ref-1)'s four-item list is AXELOS's own wording.

**Does the outline business case sit inside the brief, or beside it?** AXELOS's start-up entry lists "the
outline Business Case, Project Brief and Initiation Stage Plan" as three parallel outputs, not one nested
inside another [[1]](#ref-1). prince2.wiki names "outline business case" as part of the brief's own content
[[2]](#ref-2), prince2.ca states the same nesting explicitly [[18]](#ref-18), and Oxford City Council gives it
a numbered section of its own inside the brief template [[15]](#ref-15). This bundle carries an outline
justification and defers the full comparison to `business-case`, the reading the practitioner sources
support; AXELOS's own glossary entry is the thinner, more cautious source on this specific point.

**What does approval actually authorize, and is it the project's start?** BIS states flatly, "Approval of
the Project Brief is the official start of the project" [[6]](#ref-6). prince2.wiki and prince2.ca both
scope it narrower, to authorizing the initiation stage rather than the project itself: "whether to authorise
the initiation stage" [[2]](#ref-2), and "does not give authorization to complete the project activities"
[[18]](#ref-18). The APM consultation draft frames the same moment as "the starting point for governance to
approve the initial investment, so the project can be further defined" [[22]](#ref-22). **This is the exact
strain behind this bundle's own family placement, and this companion states this library's own POSITION on
it rather than reporting a settled fact**: approval of a project brief commits to initiating, to organized
effort finding out whether and how to proceed, not to building the product itself. The document that commits
to building is a project charter, a PID (neither built in this library), or, once the investment itself is
approved, a PRD.

**Before approval, or after?** AXELOS places the brief "pre-project" [[1]](#ref-1), and BIS makes its
approval the project's start [[6]](#ref-6). Tasmania instead prepares the brief "once the project has been
approved by the Project Sponsor and/or Project Steering Committee," to document "what is to occur in the
INITIATION Phase" [[10]](#ref-10). Tasmania is using the name for a different point in the sequence; this
bundle follows AXELOS, prince2.wiki and BIS.

**Is a project brief the same document as a project charter, a lighter version of one, or a wholly different
document?** Four positions exist in the sources read, not two. Sequenced, with narrower authority:
prince2.ca, "does not give authorization to complete the project activities" [[18]](#ref-18), and Lauren
Selley's framing of the brief as "the appetizer before the main course (your Project Charter and PID)"
[[20]](#ref-20). Different purposes, no stated sequence: Asana, "A project charter formally authorizes a
project and grants the project manager authority to allocate resources. A project brief summarizes key
details to align the team" [[14]](#ref-14). The same document, lighter: Smartsheet, "A project brief is a
simplified project charter" [[19]](#ref-19), and Knowledge Train, "the Project Charter serves a similar
purpose as the project brief" [[5]](#ref-5). Different names for one rationale: the APM consultation draft's
three interchangeable labels [[22]](#ref-22). The PMI Lexicon does not name a project brief at all
[[29]](#ref-29). This bundle adopts the first camp as a POSITION, stated as this library's own choice because
the sources genuinely split, not as a finding any one of them settles.

**Does the brief retire, or does it live on?** AXELOS, "superseded by the Project Initiation Documentation
and not maintained" [[1]](#ref-1); prince2.wiki, "no longer used in project management activities"
[[2]](#ref-2); Litten, "not used after the Initiation stage" [[31]](#ref-31). Against that, the Treasury
Board of Canada's document is resubmitted "at regular and meaningful intervals throughout the life-cycle of
the project" [[9]](#ref-9), and Ireland's NTA keeps its own under change control the whole way through
[[32]](#ref-32). The lightweight sense retires; the heavyweight sense lives on. This bundle builds the first.

**How long should it run?** See section 4; no source measured length against outcome, and the figures given
range from a paragraph to three pages.

**Skip it, or stop at it, for a small project?** See section 4; BIS and its practitioner readers give what
looks like opposite advice for the same case because they are answering two different questions, whether to
skip the brief itself, or whether to skip the heavier document that would otherwise follow it.

**Are SMART objectives required, or merely encouraged?** See section 3's Objectives subsection; sources
range from an unqualified requirement to an explicit "wherever possible" [[15]](#ref-15) to no measurability
wording at all [[16]](#ref-16).

**Is the brief a pitch?** Lauren Selley frames it as a thirty-second elevator pitch, "you're in an elevator
with the CEO of your company, and you've got just 30 seconds to pitch your project" [[20]](#ref-20). Another
practitioner source frames it instead as permission rather than persuasion: "A brief that is filled in but
never approved is a wish list, not a mandate" [[30]](#ref-30). This bundle's own boundary against a proposal
or a pitch, that a pitch sells a decision not yet made while a brief records a mandate and asks for authority
to start exploring it, is this library's own framing rather than a single named source's contrast, and is
stated as a POSITION in section 8, not as an external finding.

**Unmeasured claims, stated here as a standing caution rather than repeated as fact.** No source measured
whether writing a brief changes a project's outcome. Figures attached to an exclusions list specifically, a
stated percentage reduction in scope creep, a claim that most teams skip the section, are unmeasured
assertions from vendor content marketing, not findings; this companion states that several sources recommend
the practice and stops there.

## 7. Anti-patterns and failure modes

**Vague, unmeasurable objectives.** "Avoid vague verbs like improve, optimise or streamline, because nobody
can tell when they are done" [[30]](#ref-30). See section 3's Objectives subsection for how firmly different
sources hold this bar.

**A missing or unwritten exclusions field.** Several sources give the field its own heading precisely
because an unwritten boundary invites disputes later
[[6]](#ref-6)[[12]](#ref-12)[[15]](#ref-15). **Do not cite a specific reduction in scope creep as a measured
fact**: no source in this bundle's research measured one, and the figures that circulate attached to this
recommendation are vendor content marketing, not findings.

**A field that needs an essay.** "If a field needs an essay, that is a signal the project may need a fuller
document, not that the brief should grow" [[30]](#ref-30). A brief that keeps expanding to answer a hard
question is a signal to write the heavier document the question actually needs, not a reason to abandon the
brief's own short form.

**An unapproved draft treated as a mandate.** "A brief that is filled in but never approved is a wish list,
not a mandate; the signature is what turns it into permission to start" [[30]](#ref-30). A brief with no
named approver and no recorded answer has not done its job, whatever else it contains.

**Rushing it, or over-elaborating it, before viability is confirmed.** Litten's caution runs both directions
at once: "Avoid being rushed into completing the Project Brief too quickly, while at the same time, avoid
going into too much detail as this extra work will be wasted if the project does not continue"
[[31]](#ref-31). A brief is meant to cost little relative to the decision it supports; either failure mode
defeats that purpose.

**Treating the brief as the finished business case.** Its own outline justification is not a substitute for
the fuller comparison of options a `business-case` document brings to a later approval; a brief that tries to
settle that comparison itself has stepped outside its own boundary (`business-case_guide.md:17`).

## 8. Relationships to other artifacts

**Not a business case (purpose settled, timing contested).** The purpose boundary is solid and already
adopted on the other side of it in this library's own text: a project brief is "a short overview for
starting up a project, not an investment case," one that "absorbs an outline business case as one component
and retires once initiation documentation exists," while "the business case itself keeps being refined, it
does not retire with the brief" (`business-case_guide.md:17`; `business-case_companion.md:315-317`). The
**timing** boundary is not as clean: one vendor source states the business case is "created first, before
the project is approved" [[21]](#ref-21), but BIS's own sentence, "Approval of the Project Brief is the
official start of the project" [[6]](#ref-6), makes the brief itself the trigger a business case might
follow, not a summary of a decision already made elsewhere. Report this boundary as contested on timing and
settled only on purpose.

**Not a project charter or a PID (POSITION, where the sources split four ways).** See section 6's full
treatment of the four camps. This bundle's own POSITION treats the brief and the charter/PID as sequenced,
with narrower authority attaching to the brief's approval [[18]](#ref-18): the approved brief authorizes
initiation-stage work, not delivery of the project's products. Neither a project charter nor a PID is built
in this library, and this bundle's `project-charter` neighbour, if it is ever built, inherits nothing from
this ruling and must pass its own admission test on its own evidence.

**Not a product brief or a PRD (the weakest-evidenced boundary; labeled inferred).** No source read for this
bundle contrasts "product brief" with "project brief" by name in one sentence. Productboard describes a
product brief as capturing "the essence of a product opportunity or initiative before teams commit to
building solutions," answering "why should we work on this?" at the level of a single product opportunity
[[24]](#ref-24). This bundle's project brief instead answers why a whole initiative or project should
proceed. The product-versus-initiative distinction is inferred from triangulating the two definitions, not
cited from a source that draws the line itself, and is labeled as such.

**Not a creative or design brief (clean).** SHERPA Global draws the line by discipline: "the project brief
might be described as the bird's-eye view of the IT project," while "the creative brief defines how to best
connect with the target audience by clarifying their needs and motivations" [[25]](#ref-25). Wikipedia's own
entries corroborate from the creative and design side: a creative brief is "used by creative professionals
and agencies to develop creative deliverables, such as visual design, copy, advertising, and websites"
[[26]](#ref-26), and a design brief "outlines the deliverables and scope of the project" for a designer
working with a client, and is "adjusted as the project scope evolves" over the life of the design work
[[27]](#ref-27), unlike the project brief's own one-time, pre-commitment role.

**Not the Project Canvas (a difference of medium, not content).** Antonio Nieto-Rodriguez's Project Canvas is
"a one-page template" modeled explicitly on the Business Model Canvas, "composed of 14 dimensions" grouped
into four domains [[28]](#ref-28). No source treats a canvas as a synonym for a brief;
they differ in form, a diagram meant to be read at a glance against prose organized into sections, not in
what either one covers.

**Not a project proposal or a one-page pitch (POSITION).** Lauren Selley's elevator-pitch framing and this
practitioner's permission-to-start framing describe the same document two different ways
[[20]](#ref-20)[[30]](#ref-30). This library's own boundary, stated as a POSITION because no single source
draws it this way: a pitch sells a decision not yet made, while this bundle's brief records a mandate that
already exists and asks for authority to start exploring it further, the sense BIS's own trigger describes
[[6]](#ref-6).

**Not the NSW Health or Treasury Board of Canada heavyweight sense (named, not built).** Two named
public-sector sources publish something also called a project brief that is large, iterative and
gate-adjacent: options analysis, capital and recurrent cost estimates, a formal risk table, and resubmission
"at regular and meaningful intervals throughout the life-cycle of the project"
[[8]](#ref-8)[[9]](#ref-9)[[32]](#ref-32). This bundle builds the PRINCE2/UK BIS sense instead: short,
pre-project, superseded once initiation documentation exists. A reader who found this bundle searching for
NSW Health's or the Treasury Board of Canada's shape wants a different document, not one this library has
simply failed to build.

## 9. Adaptations

**Regulated and public-sector settings** should expect the heavyweight sense named above rather than this
bundle's shorter one: NSW Health's four-part, capital-value-scaled template [[8]](#ref-8), the Treasury Board
of Canada's iteratively resubmitted document tied to Treasury Board approval [[9]](#ref-9), and Ireland's
NTA gateway process, where the brief is kept under change control and only its approved version lets the
next phase begin [[32]](#ref-32).

**Small projects** get an explicit scaling instruction from the primary source itself: "For small projects
this will be a very short document often with only a few sentences for each section" [[6]](#ref-6), and, on
whether the document is needed at all, see section 4's live disagreement rather than a single rule.

**Organisations governed by PRINCE2** should treat the brief as a genuinely temporary artifact: produced once
during starting up a project, consulted once at the initiation-stage decision, and then superseded, never
revised in place the way a business case or a PID would be [[1]](#ref-1)[[2]](#ref-2)[[31]](#ref-31).

**Teams that already default to a project charter** should read section 6 and section 8 before assuming the
two documents are interchangeable at their own organisation; three of the four camps this research found
treat them as genuinely different instruments, not the same form under two names.

## 10. Worked example

See `project-brief_example.md`. Per this library's `discovery-docs` family contract, the example chains onto
the shared Acme Analytics scenario, dated before the business case it leads to: a project brief for the
Question-First Entry and Modelling Defaults initiative, asking Dana Okoro, VP Product, for authorization to
proceed to a funding decision. It absorbs the same trend in Acme's own telemetry that later justifies the
business case in full, and it carries an outline estimate of time and cost labeled illustrative rather than
final, consistent with this section's own boundary against the fuller comparison `business-case` performs.
