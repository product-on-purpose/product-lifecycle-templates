# Problem Statement: the companion

The reference behind the template. Read this when you want to know why a section exists, what the
research actually says, and where the sources genuinely disagree.

## 1. Orientation

A problem statement names which problem is worth solving, who has it, and what evidence shows it is
real, before anyone names a solution. Nielsen Norman Group's own definition is the one this bundle
builds from: "A concise description of the problem that needs to be solved" [[1]](#ref-1). It is written
early, at the start of a discovery, and it is deliberately short: NHS Wales's own guide puts a typical
instance at "about 18 words long" [[15]](#ref-15), and Nielsen Norman Group's own brevity rule is "Be
brief" [[1]](#ref-1). Epec's own framing of a problem in general, not of this document specifically, is
worth holding in mind while reading the rest of this companion: "A problem is merely the difference
between the way things are and the way things need to be" [[24]](#ref-24).

At a glance:

- **This is a short document at both sizes.** The full variant adds four sections that recur across
  independent sectioned forms this research read; it does not turn the statement into a business case,
  and options, a recommended solution, a cost estimate, a project scope and a funding request stay out
  at every size.
- **The overlap with this library's `business-case` bundle is real, and this companion says so plainly.**
  `business-case`'s Problem and Opportunity section asks the same questions inside an investment case.
  The standalone statement comes earlier, asks for no money, and decides whether a problem is worth a
  project brief or a business case at all. That framing is this library's own POSITION; no source read
  for this bundle contrasts the two documents by name. See section 8.
- **A root cause stays out of the statement itself.** NHS Wales and Epec both exclude it; two other
  sources invite root-cause thinking into the surrounding exercise. This bundle keeps a suspected cause
  out of the Problem Statement section and gives it a labeled, hypothesis-only place in Open Questions
  and Assumptions, following the A3 template's own separation. That placement is a POSITION, not a
  point every source agrees on. See sections 3 and 6.
- **The opportunity statement is the same artefact, phrased positively, with one dissent on record.**
  Nielsen Norman Group names it an alias used "in the same way" [[1]](#ref-1); one business-analysis
  source argues the two are not interchangeable for projects [[28]](#ref-28). See section 6.
- **No two sources this research read share a section order or a section count.** This companion names
  whose list is whose throughout, rather than attributing any one composition generically to "the"
  canon.

## 2. Origins and evolution

There is no single named originator of "problem statement" as a written artefact. Instead, several
independent lineages converge on something like it, and this bundle's canon sources sit in at least
three of them.

**UX discovery practice** is the lineage this bundle leans on hardest. Nielsen Norman Group's Maria
Rosala places the document "In the discovery phase of a UX project," used "to identify and frame the
problem to be explored and solved, as well as to communicate the discovery's scope and focus"
[[1]](#ref-1). The article carries its own freshness marker, "August 22, 2021 · Last reviewed Sep. 2,
2026" [[1]](#ref-1).

**Government service design** is a second, independent lineage, and it does not borrow Nielsen Norman
Group's vocabulary. New South Wales's Digital Service Toolkit tells teams to "defin[e] the problem you're
working on" before exploring solutions and to "avoid relying on assumptions" [[2]](#ref-2). The UK's
GOV.UK Service Manual describes the same move in its own words, reframing a handed-down solution "as a
problem to be solved" [[3]](#ref-3), in a page last updated "21 June 2021" [[3]](#ref-3). Singapore's
Build for Good guide and the US General Services Administration's digital.gov both publish parallel,
independently worded guidance in the same government tradition [[4]](#ref-4)[[5]](#ref-5). None of these
four pages mentions Nielsen Norman Group or its article, in the text this research read.

**Design thinking** supplies an adjacent but separately named construct, the Point of View, rather than
the same term. Stanford's d.school CS377 course handout dates the construct to "September 2006"
[[6]](#ref-6) and gives it its own formula, "User + Need = Point of View" [[6]](#ref-6). Even within
design thinking, naming is not universal: the Design Council's own Framework for Innovation, the Double
Diamond, names four stages, "Discover," "Define," "Develop" and "Deliver" [[7]](#ref-7), and never names
a written Define-stage deliverable at all. That is a negative finding worth holding onto: the stage this
library maps a problem statement to does not itself require a document called a problem statement, even
in a widely cited design-thinking framework.

**What this research could not read.** Four fetch attempts against McKinsey's own problem-statement
worksheet timed out, so no McKinsey field name appears anywhere in this bundle. Atlassian's companion
Confluence and Trello templates open inside application shells this research could not enter, so only
the public play page is cited here. Two Harvard Business Review articles, from 2012 and 2017, are paywalled, a
job-story post and a Lean UX hypothesis-statement excerpt could not be fetched, and PMI's own
problem-statement page returned HTTP 403. None of those sources appears anywhere in this companion.

## 3. Anatomy (section by section)

### Problem Statement (lean and full)

The statement itself: a few sentences, from the affected person's perspective, naming no solution.
Nielsen Norman Group's definition anchors it, "A concise description of the problem that needs to be
solved" [[1]](#ref-1), and NHS Wales adds the brevity bar, "about 18 words long" [[15]](#ref-15).
Atlassian's own Problem Framing play names the same four elements this section asks for: "who is
affected, what is affecting them, why it needs to be solved, and where the problem is happening"
[[11]](#ref-11). Heap's own worksheet uses the identical section label, "Problem Statement. What pain
point(s) are we trying to address?" [[8]](#ref-8).

**Beginner note.** Write this section last, even though it reads first. Two government sources give the
worked move away from a handed-down solution: Digital NSW rewrites "We need to build an app to show
people where they can buy cheap fuel" into "Drivers are spending more time and money than they're saving
looking for cheap fuel" [[2]](#ref-2), and GOV.UK performs the identical move on its own example,
rewriting an interactive-map solution into "How can we make it easier for people to find their nearest
contact centre" [[3]](#ref-3). Nielsen Norman Group notes that "many teams will compose their problem
statement in a discovery kick-off workshop" [[1]](#ref-1), after the background and evidence sections
below already have a first draft.

**Expert note.** Keep it to one problem. Nielsen Norman Group's own quality rule reads, "Not be a laundry
list of unrelated problems. A discovery effort should have one problem statement" [[1]](#ref-1), and NHS
Wales's checklist reinforces the same bar from the other direction, "Doesn't indicate a solution or a
cause" [[15]](#ref-15).

### Background (lean and full)

Where the problem shows up, how it came to attention, and what is affecting the people who have it.
Nielsen Norman Group names the question directly, "Which organization or department has the problem and
what is the problem? Why has the problem arisen?" [[1]](#ref-1), and Atlassian's own prompts ask "What is
the nature of the problem? What signs point to there being a problem?" [[11]](#ref-11).

**Beginner note.** Answer the "which organization or department" question even when it feels obvious;
the next reader of the document may not share that context.

**Expert note.** Keep this section narrative and the Evidence section below quantified. A background
paragraph that restates the same sentence the Evidence section should carry as data is a common way this
section drifts into padding.

### Who Is Affected (lean and full)

The people who have the problem, named specifically enough to find them. Nielsen Norman Group's own
wording leaves room for more than one group, "There could be multiple user groups affected by a specific
problem in different ways" [[1]](#ref-1), and Atlassian asks the verification question directly, "Who is
experiencing this problem? How do we know that they are experiencing it?" [[11]](#ref-11). NHS Scotland's
Problem Framer tool carries a closely related, but separately labeled, field, "Who The Problem Most
Affects" [[14]](#ref-14); the same tool also names "Who The Problem Benefits" [[14]](#ref-14) as a
distinct field, which this bundle does not carry as a heading of its own.

**Beginner note.** Where this library's `user-persona` bundle already exists for the group in question,
name the persona by name rather than re-describing the group from scratch.

**Expert note.** If the statement serves more than one group differently, say so explicitly rather than
folding them into one generic "users" line; Nielsen Norman Group's own wording treats that as the normal
case, not the exception [[1]](#ref-1).

### Impact and Why Now (lean and full)

What the problem costs the people who have it and the organization, and why it matters now rather than
later. Nielsen Norman Group's own prompt runs, "If the problem is not fixed, what will be the effect on
the organization? Reputational damage? Paying unavoidable costs? Losing out-of-market share?"
[[1]](#ref-1), and Atlassian asks it from the affected person's side, "What happens if we don't solve it?"
[[11]](#ref-11). GOV.UK adds the instruction to size the impact where possible, "You should also consider
quantifying the value of solving the problem" [[3]](#ref-3).

**Beginner note.** A rough, directional number belongs here. Two unrelated sources show the contrast
between a vague and a quantified impact line: one newsletter's own worked pair runs from "Onboarding is
too slow and people don't like it" to "new starters take six weeks to complete onboarding" and "we're
spending £12,000 per cohort on travel and venue costs" [[31]](#ref-31).

**Expert note.** Keep the figure directional, not a costed case. A Six Sigma source draws the line this
section stops short of: "the problem statement describes what, where, and how much, without the
persuasive framing," while "a specific dollar estimate, even a rough one" belongs to a business case
[[30]](#ref-30). A full costed estimate with its own methodology is this library's `business-case`
bundle's job, not this one's.

### Evidence (lean and full)

What shows the problem is real, where each piece came from, and how strong it is. Digital NSW states the
obligation plainly, "Use the evidence you gather to document your clearly defined problem"
[[2]](#ref-2), and NHS Wales states the cost of skipping it, "without evidence of your problem, you are
left with opinion" [[15]](#ref-15). Singapore's Build for Good guide shows the contrast directly, pairing
a quantified example, "60% of Singaporeans lack the knowledge of what can be recycled when disposing
their trash," against a weaker unquantified one, "Singaporeans find it too troublesome to recycle"
[[4]](#ref-4).

**Beginner note.** Cite where each piece of evidence came from. Digital NSW's own review question is a
useful final check, "Is this based on evidence rather than assumptions?" [[2]](#ref-2).

**Expert note.** Name the extent of the problem, not only its existence. A Six Sigma source adds the
magnitude question this library's canon sources leave implicit: "To emphasize the significance of the
problem, it is essential to explain the extent or magnitude of the issue" [[29]](#ref-29), with "data or
qualitative descriptions that reveal the seriousness and widespread nature of the problem"
[[29]](#ref-29).

### How People Cope Today (full only)

How the people who have the problem work around it now, and what has already been tried and why it did
not work, held as evidence and never as an option. Heap's own worksheet asks, "How do people currently
solve this problem?" [[8]](#ref-8), and its author restates the same question as part of a wider
framing exercise, "how customers work around the problem" [[10]](#ref-10). A University of Waterloo
submission form asks for the same history under its own "Failure analysis" field, "Describe previous
attempts to solve the problem and why they failed" [[32]](#ref-32).

**Beginner note.** Describe the workaround at the level a reader needs to judge how painful it is, not
in enough detail that it starts to read as a proposed fix.

**Expert note.** This section is evidence of weight, not an options list. A workaround described in
implementation detail has crossed into solution territory and belongs to a later document; keep the
description to what people do and why it falls short.

### What Is Not Part of the Problem (full only)

Neighbouring problems this statement deliberately leaves out, agreed before anyone scopes work against
it. GOV.UK names the move directly, "Reframing the problem also includes agreeing what is not part of
the problem" [[3]](#ref-3), and a Rose-Hulman course template asks for "the scope of the project, what is
to be included and, perhaps more importantly, what isn't" [[16]](#ref-16). Atlassian's own prompt asks
the boundary from the other side, "In what circumstances does it not occur?" [[11]](#ref-11).

**Beginner note.** Name at least one neighbouring problem this statement is deliberately not about.

**Expert note.** This is not a project scope section; a project's scope, options and plan stay out of
this bundle at every size. This section draws the boundary of the problem itself, not of the work that
might follow if the problem is pursued.

### How We Will Know It Is Solved (full only)

Observable signals that the problem has shrunk, stated without naming a solution: the measure, its
current level, and the direction it needs to move. Jeff Gothelf's Business Problem Statement formula
closes on exactly this question, "as determined by [these measurable changes in their behavior]"
[[12]](#ref-12), and his own walkthrough of the Lean UX Canvas restates it as the canvas box's final
piece, "if we solve it, how we will we know? ... what customer behavior or metric shift will we see?"
[[13]](#ref-13). Heap's worksheet gives it a labeled field, "Metric(s) and Baseline: Define how you'll
evaluate success" [[8]](#ref-8), and a Rose-Hulman template asks for "a summary of the primary success
criteria" [[16]](#ref-16).

**Beginner note.** Name the measure and its current level; leave the target number for the document
that proposes how to move it.

**Expert note, and a named contest.** Whether success measures belong in the problem statement at all is
genuinely disputed, not settled. Queensland's own "Defining success" play treats it as separate, later
work that assumes the problem is already settled, "Make sure you have a clearly defined problem ... You
should already have a well-defined problem" [[21]](#ref-21), and draws its own line against this
section's content, "The outcome is not an output or a metric of your project" [[21]](#ref-21). This
bundle carries the section in the full variant and names the split rather than treating either camp as
the only one. Separately, one Six Sigma source recommends going further still and folding in "a claim or
working thesis that presents a potential solution or approach" [[29]](#ref-29); this bundle's no-solution
rule, held across every other canon source this research read, overrides that one recommendation.

### Open Questions and Assumptions (full only)

What is not yet known, what is being assumed true until research says otherwise, and a root cause the
team suspects, labeled as a hypothesis. Heap's worksheet carries a literal "Open Questions" field
[[8]](#ref-8), and a Canada School of Public Service job aid states the underlying caution directly: a
statement "done before doing design research and consulting stakeholders should be considered as being
based on assumptions" [[33]](#ref-33).

**Beginner note.** List what the statement is assuming, not only what it asserts as fact; Nielsen Norman
Group names refining the statement "if you've learned root causes or how much a problem costs your
organization" as a normal, later move [[1]](#ref-1), not a sign the first draft was wrong.

**Expert note, and a POSITION.** Whether a root cause belongs anywhere near this document splits the
sources. NHS Wales states plainly that "problem statements do not describe the solution or root causes"
[[15]](#ref-15), and a 5 Whys guide goes further, that a good statement "does not attempt to diagnose the
problem, nor assign blame" [[24]](#ref-24). Against that, Digital NSW's own review checklist asks "Do we
understand the root cause?" [[2]](#ref-2). The University of South Carolina's A3 template resolves the
tension by keeping the two apart structurally: the problem statement is step 1, and "What is your most
likely root cause?" is a separate, later step 5 [[34]](#ref-34). This bundle follows that separation. A
suspected cause goes here, labeled a hypothesis; the Problem Statement section itself names none. digital.gov's
own definition of the term is worth having on hand while writing this section: a root cause is "the
fundamental reason for the occurrence of a problem" [[5]](#ref-5). The same source's own caution about
framing applies directly to this document, "how we define a problem largely defines its solution"
[[5]](#ref-5), which is exactly why a suspected cause belongs here as a flagged guess, not stated as
settled fact anywhere else in the document.

### Decision This Informs (lean and full)

Who decides whether this problem is worth pursuing further, by when, and which document takes over if
the answer is yes. GOV.UK frames the decision itself, "Your discovery is finished when you've decided
whether or not you want to move on to alpha," adding that "it's not a failure to stop at the end of the
discovery phase" [[3]](#ref-3), and Digital NSW's own checklist ends on the same question, "Is the
problem clear enough to investigate further?" [[2]](#ref-2). One practitioner reading of PMBOK describes
the moment after approval in the same terms this section asks for: "After the problem statement is
created and approved, it's time to move on to possible solutions" [[26]](#ref-26).

**Beginner note.** Name the decider and the date by which they need to answer, not only the question
itself.

**Expert note, and a POSITION.** Which document takes over is this library's own call, not a rule any
source read for this bundle states. This library's `discovery-docs` family contract frames every member
as "written once for a decision, and finished when that decision is made"
(`docs/internal/contracts/discovery-docs.md`, section on membership), and the same contract's
shared-scenario rule names this bundle as the one that "frames the problem that the project brief and
the business case later answer" (`docs/internal/contracts/discovery-docs.md`, section 4). Name the
specific document, a project brief, a business case, or both in sequence, that this particular decision
leads to, rather than leaving the pointer generic.

## 4. Variants and sizing

**Lean** carries Problem Statement, Background, Who Is Affected, Impact and Why Now, Evidence, and
Decision This Informs: enough to frame the problem, show it is real, and name the decision it feeds,
without adding sectioned structure beyond what this bundle's shortest sources describe. **Full** inserts
How People Cope Today, What Is Not Part of the Problem, How We Will Know It Is Solved, and Open Questions
and Assumptions, in that order, between Evidence and Decision This Informs.

Brevity is the convention at both sizes, not only the lean one. NHS Wales puts a typical instance at
"about 18 words long" [[15]](#ref-15), Nielsen Norman Group's own brevity rule reads, "If you can condense
your problem statement down to a few sentences, others will quickly understand what you focus on and why,
and what's out of scope" [[1]](#ref-1), and the Stanford d.school handout states the same expectation for
its own adjacent construct, the Point of View: "Great ones can be compressed to fit on a bumper sticker"
[[6]](#ref-6). None of the sources this research read describing a longer, sectioned form abandons that
brevity convention for the headline statement itself; the full variant adds named fields around it, not
length to it.

**Each full-only section rests on a source this research actually read**, never on an invented structure:
How People Cope Today recurs in Heap's worksheet, Heap's own author and a University of Waterloo
submission form [[8]](#ref-8)[[10]](#ref-10)[[32]](#ref-32); What Is Not Part of the Problem recurs in
GOV.UK, a Rose-Hulman template and Atlassian's play [[3]](#ref-3)[[16]](#ref-16)[[11]](#ref-11); How We
Will Know It Is Solved recurs in Gothelf's two sources, Heap's worksheet and the same Rose-Hulman
template, contested by Queensland's own playbook [[12]](#ref-12)[[13]](#ref-13)[[8]](#ref-8)
[[16]](#ref-16)[[21]](#ref-21); and Open Questions and Assumptions recurs in Heap's worksheet, the Canada
School of Public Service job aid and the University of South Carolina's A3 template
[[8]](#ref-8)[[33]](#ref-33)[[34]](#ref-34).

**One field has no sourced place in this bundle at any size.** No source this research read for this
bundle labels a constraints section; the nearest candidate, NHS Scotland's own "Project Barriers" field
[[14]](#ref-14), names a barrier met along the way, not a limit accepted before the work starts, and this
bundle does not adopt it.

## 5. Methodology lineage

Three lineages this research read arrive at the artefact separately: no page read from one lineage mentions
a source from another, and design thinking names its construct differently. **UX discovery
practice**, through Nielsen Norman Group [[1]](#ref-1), is the fullest single source this bundle leans
on for required parts. **Government service design**, through New South Wales, GOV.UK, Singapore's Build
for Good and the US GSA's digital.gov, converges independently on the same solution-to-problem reframing
move without naming "problem statement" consistently across all four
[[2]](#ref-2)[[3]](#ref-3)[[4]](#ref-4)[[5]](#ref-5). **Design thinking** supplies the Point of View as an
adjacent, separately named construct, formalized at Stanford's d.school [[6]](#ref-6)[[17]](#ref-17), and
feeding forward into How Might We questions as the next move after the problem is framed: "HMW is the
bridge between the Define and Ideate stages of the design thinking process" [[18]](#ref-18), and "Step 1:
Clearly define the problem or challenge with a concise problem statement" is explicitly named as the
input HMW questions need [[18]](#ref-18). IDEO.org's own method page describes the same handoff from its
side, "reframing your insight statements as How Might We questions to turn those challenges into
opportunities for design" [[19]](#ref-19).

**Product management and Lean UX** form a fourth lineage, through Heap's worksheet series and Jeff
Gothelf's Lean UX Canvas. Heap positions its own worksheet as the first of three, "we've put together
three worksheets to help you implement a repeatable process for getting to the why" [[9]](#ref-9), and
calls it "A worksheet for defining product problems" [[9]](#ref-9); its own named author, in contrast,
describes the same artefact as "a document that lays out the problem to be solved and argues for why we
should choose to solve this particular problem" [[10]](#ref-10). The worksheet label alone does not
settle which of those two descriptions is more accurate. Gothelf's own formula sits inside a single box
of a larger canvas, "We'll start off with the business problem statement. That's box number 1"
[[13]](#ref-13), rather than as a standalone sectioned document.

**Project management and continuous improvement** form a fifth, more contested lineage. One project
management source places the document "at the very start of the project lifecycle, during the initiation
phase, before the project charter is drafted" [[20]](#ref-20), listing "root cause analysis" among its own
contents [[20]](#ref-20); against that, the A3 problem-solving tradition from Lean and continuous
improvement keeps root-cause analysis as a separate, later, explicitly hypothesis-framed step
[[34]](#ref-34). Two standards-body sources are nearly silent on the term itself: the Association for
Project Management's own glossary has no "problem statement" entry at all, defining "Brief" as "The output
of the concept phase of a project or programme" and "Charter" as "A document that sets out the working
relationships and agreed behaviours within a project team," two different things [[25]](#ref-25); and one
practitioner's reading of the PMBOK Guide reports its only mentions sit under a cause-and-effect diagram
in the 5th edition and under root-cause data analysis in the 6th, never tied to a named initiation
artefact [[26]](#ref-26).

## 6. Debates and contested boundaries

**Do success measures belong in the problem statement?** Gothelf's two sources, Heap's worksheet, a
Rose-Hulman template and one project-management source all fold measurable criteria into the document
itself [[12]](#ref-12)[[13]](#ref-13)[[8]](#ref-8)[[16]](#ref-16)[[20]](#ref-20); Queensland's own
"Defining success" play treats defining success as separate, later work that assumes "you should already
have a well-defined problem" [[21]](#ref-21), and NHS Wales's deliberately short, 18-word instance has no
room for one [[15]](#ref-15). This bundle carries the section in the full variant only and names the
split here rather than resolving it as settled.

**Written once, or revisited?** One project-management source places it once, "at the very start of the
project lifecycle" [[20]](#ref-20). Against that, the Stanford d.school handout treats its own adjacent
construct as iterative, "You revisit and reformulate as you learn by doing" [[17]](#ref-17), and Nielsen
Norman Group states the same for the problem statement directly, "Throughout the discovery process, you
can return to your problem statement and add to it" [[1]](#ref-1). This bundle follows the
`discovery-docs` family contract's own cadence clause: written once for the decision it opens, and
refined during the discovery that decision launches, until the decision is made
(`docs/internal/contracts/discovery-docs.md`, section on membership).

**Whose problem is it?** The design-thinking Point of View formula is explicitly user-centred, "[USER]
needs to [USER'S NEED] because [SURPRISING INSIGHT]" [[17]](#ref-17), and the Interaction Design
Foundation's own account of the related How Might We formula centres a "Potential User" the same way
[[18]](#ref-18). Gothelf's Business Problem Statement instead starts from the business side, a product
that "isn't meeting our expectations" [[12]](#ref-12). Nielsen Norman Group carries both: its required
parts name "The people affected by the problem" and, separately, "The impact of the problem on the
organization" [[1]](#ref-1). This bundle carries both, in Who Is Affected and in Impact and Why Now.

**Does a root cause belong anywhere near this document?** See section 3's Open Questions and Assumptions
subsection for the full treatment. NHS Wales and a 5 Whys guide exclude it from the statement
[[15]](#ref-15)[[24]](#ref-24); Digital NSW's own checklist invites it into the surrounding exercise
[[2]](#ref-2); the A3 template keeps it as a separate later step [[34]](#ref-34), and this bundle follows
that separation as a POSITION.

**Is the opportunity statement the same document?** Nielsen Norman Group calls it an alias used "in the
same way" as a problem statement that happens to capture an opportunity instead [[1]](#ref-1). Gothelf
publishes a separate Business Opportunity Statement formula for a new initiative, and recommends trying
the problem-statement form first, "My advice is to try the Business Problem Statement first. If it
doesn't make sense after you've given it a shot, go for the Opportunity Statement" [[12]](#ref-12). One
business-analysis source dissents outright for projects specifically: "many practitioners treat problems
and opportunities interchangeably when developing Business Cases and Project Charters" [[28]](#ref-28),
treating the conflation as a named error rather than a harmless alias. This bundle follows Nielsen Norman
Group's reading as the alias and reports the dissent here rather than silently picking a side.

**Is Atlassian's Problem Framing play a document or a workshop?** Its own tagline frames the activity, not
a deliverable: "the goal today is to understand and define the problem, not to solve it"
[[11]](#ref-11). Its output is still a written statement that "should include who is affected, what is
affecting them, why it needs to be solved, and where the problem is happening" [[11]](#ref-11). This
companion cites the play for those four elements, never for a document layout; its own editable template
was gated and was not read.

**Does the problem statement precede other documents, or live inside them?** This bundle builds it as a
standalone precursor, consistent with one project-management reading that treats it as written first and
feeding what follows [[26]](#ref-26). Elsewhere, the same content commonly lives as one section inside a
larger document: Productboard's own glossary names "Problem Statement" as the first numbered section of a
product brief, "A clear, concise description of the user problem or business opportunity. Avoid solution
language; focus on the pain point or unmet need" [[27]](#ref-27), and this library's own `prd` companion
already names "a problem statement" as one of several things that "precede" a PRD
(`templates/prd/prd_companion.md:141`). Both forms exist; this bundle ships the standalone precursor, and
the component form stays inside the documents that already carry it.

## 7. Anti-patterns and failure modes

**A solution in disguise.** Named by government and UX sources alike. Digital NSW and GOV.UK both publish the identical corrective move, rewriting a
handed-down solution as a problem [[2]](#ref-2)[[3]](#ref-3); Build for Good states the rule directly, "Do
not include solutions in your problem statement" [[4]](#ref-4); and a UX design guide explains why it
matters, a user "doesn't need to be able to use two-factor authentication to get into a banking app, they
need to be able to access their banking information securely" [[22]](#ref-22).

**Scope set too broad or too narrow.** A UX design guide states the tension directly: "if it's too broad,
it will leave too many possible solutions open... it should be broad enough to allow for creative thinking
and innovative solutions, but narrow enough that it can provide a direction for that solution"
[[22]](#ref-22). A separate practitioner source names the vague-audience version of the same failure,
"Many people provide vague problem statements that can confuse UX designers... specify the target
audience, the platform, and the specific scenarios in which the problem occurs" [[23]](#ref-23).

**Opinion standing in for evidence.** NHS Wales's own warning is blunt, "without evidence of your problem,
you are left with opinion" [[15]](#ref-15); Digital NSW's review checklist asks the same question as a
gate, "Is this based on evidence rather than assumptions?" [[2]](#ref-2).

**Blame or a predetermined cause dressed up as a problem statement.** A 5 Whys guide names this precisely:
a good statement "does not offer commentary on a proposed solution. It does not attempt to diagnose the
problem, nor assign blame. It is objective and factual" [[24]](#ref-24), contrasting it with a bad example
that smuggles in both a diagnosis and a fix, "Workers are making mistakes because there is no work
instruction" [[24]](#ref-24).

**A laundry list of unrelated problems.** Nielsen Norman Group's own rule: "A discovery effort should have
one problem statement, and the problem statement should be focused on one problem" [[1]](#ref-1).

**One neutral statement smoothing over genuinely different stakeholder views.** A Canada School of Public
Service job aid writes a point-of-view problem statement once per stakeholder rather than merging them
into a single voice, illustrated from one inspector's own perspective: "I am an inspector and I am trying
to document a case of non-compliance, but I cannot take pictures because my device is not secure, which
makes me feel vulnerable and inefficient" [[33]](#ref-33). Where stakeholders genuinely disagree on what
the problem is, write more than one statement rather than flattening the disagreement into a false
consensus.

**Stopping the 5 Whys too early or too late.** A UX design guide treats the number five as a convention,
not a rule: "the number five is only a rule of thumb... you should stop asking why once your answers are
no longer valuable in defining the problem" [[22]](#ref-22), and warns that a question with more than one
legitimate answer "could reveal your user has more than one problem" [[22]](#ref-22) rather than a reason
to pick one answer and move on.

## 8. Relationships to other artifacts

**Not `business-case` (the duplication is real, named here directly).** `business-case`'s own Problem and
Opportunity section asks the same questions this bundle asks, inside an investment case. The standalone
statement this bundle ships comes earlier, asks for no money, and decides whether a problem is worth a
project brief or a business case at all; a business case written afterward can cite the statement and
keep its own problem section shorter. That framing is this library's own POSITION: no source read for
this bundle contrasts the two documents by name.

**Not `project-brief` (narrower than one source first suggested).** An Association for Project Management
consultation draft, read for this library's `project-brief` research, names "problem statement," "project
brief" and "project charter" as interchangeable labels for one early rationale
(`templates/project-brief/project-brief_research-log.md:156`). This research read APM's own published
glossary directly, and it carries no "problem statement" entry at all, defining "Brief" as "The output of
the concept phase of a project or programme" and "Charter" as "A document that sets out the working
relationships and agreed behaviours within a project team," two different things [[25]](#ref-25). The two
readings do not contradict each other outright, since a body-of-knowledge passage can mention a name its
own glossary does not define, but this companion attributes only what the glossary itself says: a project
brief asks a named approver for authority to start initiation, where this bundle's statement asks for no
authority and names no project at all.

**Not the PRD, and not a duplicate of its own component form.** This library's own `prd` companion already
names "a problem statement" as one of several things that "precede" a PRD
(`templates/prd/prd_companion.md:141`), and the same content commonly lives as one section inside a
product brief, Productboard's own first numbered section [[27]](#ref-27). This bundle ships the standalone
precursor; where a document already carries the content as a section of itself, that component form is
not displaced by this bundle existing alongside it.

**An alias, not a neighbour: the opportunity statement.** See section 6's full treatment of the alias and
its one dissent [[1]](#ref-1)[[12]](#ref-12)[[28]](#ref-28).

**Precedes How Might We, a job aid never shared with it.** The Interaction Design Foundation's own
sequencing places a problem statement before any How Might We question is written, "Step 1: Clearly define
the problem or challenge with a concise problem statement to capture the core of the problem. This sets
the foundation for HMW questions" [[18]](#ref-18). A How Might We question already points toward
generating solutions, "Now, try reframing your insight statements as How Might We questions to turn those
challenges into opportunities for design" [[19]](#ref-19); this bundle's statement stops one step short of
that, naming no solution and no reframe.

## 9. Adaptations

**UX discovery teams** have the fullest single template to work from, Nielsen Norman Group's own required
parts and its 5 Ws technique for gathering them, which asks "Who is affected by the problem?", "What is
the problem?", "Where does this problem occur?", "When does the problem occur?" and "Why does the problem
occur?" [[1]](#ref-1).

**Government service teams** should expect the solution-to-problem rewrite move to be the central skill,
not the section list: Digital NSW's and GOV.UK's worked examples are the clearest templates for that
specific move [[2]](#ref-2)[[3]](#ref-3), and Singapore's Build for Good adds its own concreteness bar, "Be
concrete" and "Be specific" [[4]](#ref-4).

**Design-thinking teams** already running a Point of View exercise can treat this bundle's Problem
Statement section as compatible with, rather than a replacement for, that formula, and should read section
5's lineage note on how the two constructs relate before writing both.

**Lean UX and product teams** already using a canvas can keep Gothelf's own Business Problem Statement
formula as the box-1 phrasing and still use this bundle's fuller sections, Background, Who Is Affected,
Evidence, for the detail the single canvas box does not have room for [[12]](#ref-12)[[13]](#ref-13).

**Project-management-leaning teams** that place this document before a charter, following one source's own
sequencing [[20]](#ref-20), should still keep root-cause analysis out of the Problem Statement section
itself and into Open Questions and Assumptions, per section 3 and section 6's treatment of that split.

**Continuous-improvement and Lean Six Sigma teams** already running an A3 can treat this bundle's section
order as compatible with the A3's own first step, and should carry the A3's own later, separate root-cause
step forward rather than merging it back into the statement [[34]](#ref-34).

## 10. Worked example

See `problem-statement_example.md`. Per this library's `discovery-docs` family contract, the example
chains onto the shared Acme Analytics scenario, dated before both documents it leads to: a problem
statement for the problem that the Question-First Entry project brief and business case later answer. It
cites only facts already true when it is dated, names no solution, and does not cite the project brief,
the business case, or anything else dated after it, as already existing.
