## Honest framing (the through-line for the companion)

**1. The document is unstudied, and that is a tested negative rather than an untested assumption.** There is
a real academic literature on whether strategic *planning as a process* correlates with firm performance,
and it is genuinely mixed: two meta-analyses find a positive, context-moderated relationship [14][16], and
at least one national study of listed firms finds none on objective measures while finding one on
*subjective* measures [17]. That last result is the sharpest thing in the file: managers believed planning
helped when the financial data said it did not. Nothing found measures a **written product strategy
document** against product or firm outcomes. The nearest study scores strategic *coherence* from interviews,
explicitly not an artifact [18]. This bundle therefore claims no performance benefit for writing a strategy
down, and says why it can defend that.

**2. Product strategy is a layer product management bolted onto an older hierarchy.** The classical
corporate/business/functional model [6] has no product layer, and the founding corporate-strategy text [5]
does not contain one. Cagan puts product strategy directly beneath business strategy [26]; Pichler inserts a
portfolio layer between them [32]. No source reconciles these. The bundle picks one lineage and says so
rather than implying a consensus that does not exist.

**3. The whole field agrees on the failure mode and disagrees on the shape.** Every serious source, from
Porter in 1996 [4] to a 2026 newsletter [63], says the same thing about what makes a strategy real: it names
what you will not do. Rumelt calls its absence "mistaking goals for strategy" [1], Porter calls trade-offs
"essential" [4], Doshi tests for named non-target segments [59], Traynor tests whether the team can say "great
idea, we're not building it" [61], Kantus tests whether anyone is uncomfortable [63]. That convergence, from
authors who are not citing each other, is the strongest claim this bundle can make.

**4. No canonical origin exists for the product strategy document as an artifact.** Wikipedia's own article
makes no origin claim [69]. This is reported as *not verified to exist*, not as a confirmed absence.

**[1] Richard Rumelt - Good Strategy Bad Strategy (2011), quoted via a reader-curated quotations page.**

Quotable: "The kernel of a strategy contains three elements: a diagnosis, a guiding policy, and coherent action."

Contested/time-bound: this is a reader-curated quotations page, not Rumelt's or his publisher's site. No official excerpt could be located. Wording matches across many independent summaries, but treat punctuation as transcribed. The fourth hallmark ("bad strategic objectives") and its "dog's dinner" and "blue sky" sub-types are widely repeated and were **not** verified verbatim anywhere; do not quote them.

## Claims flagged contested or time-bound

1. **Does strategic planning improve performance?** Positive in two meta-analyses [14][16], null on objective
   measures in [17]. Genuinely unresolved. The bundle must not present either side as settled.
2. **Does a written product strategy document improve outcomes?** Nothing found measures it. The search that
   establishes this is recorded: direct queries for the document as an independent variable returned only
   adjacent literatures on strategy *choices* and on planning *processes*. This is a confirmed gap, not an
   unverified absence.
3. **Rumelt's chain of custody.** The 2011 McKinsey essay [3] was never retrieved despite six attempts across
   three dimensions. The book itself was read only through a reader-curated quotations page [1] and two
   second-hand renderings [66][71]. The fourth bad-strategy hallmark and its "dog's dinner" and "blue sky"
   sub-types were never verified verbatim and are **not quoted** in this bundle.
4. **"The crux" is not 2011 vocabulary.** It belongs to Rumelt's 2022 follow-up. At least one explainer [67]
   blends it into the kernel.
5. **"Product Strategy Canvas" is not Roman Pichler's.** His site publishes no tool under that name; a
   framework catalog attributes it to Melissa Perri's framework with the canvas by Alex Pedicini [42]. The
   attribution is unresolved, so the bundle avoids the name.
6. **Melissa Perri's 2016 and 2018 structures differ** [39][41], and the book was not read. Do not present
   one as the other.
7. **Levels of strategy are contested at the product boundary.** Classical strategy has three levels and no
   product layer [6]; Cagan puts product strategy directly under business strategy [26]; Pichler inserts a
   portfolio layer [32]. No source reconciles them.
8. **Document length has no evidence behind it.** Amazon standardises on six narrative pages [8]; Kao argues
   for one [45]. No survey or corpus study of length was found. Any page count is judgment.
9. **Cadence versus reactivity read alike and are not.** Quarterly *review* is endorsed [32][62]; quarterly
   *pivots in reaction to competitors* is listed as theater [63]. State both precisely.
10. **"Strategy theater" is not an established term.** Searched; found one newsletter using it [63] and the
    better-established sibling "innovation theater" elsewhere. The academic parallel studies workshops, not
    documents [13]. Use the phrase as one author's coinage, not as a field-wide critique.
11. **No canonical origin for the artifact.** Not verified to exist [69]. Do not assert one.
12. **A3 and Hoshin Kanri are not product strategy formats** on this evidence [68]. Real methods, no named
    product-management practitioner publishing them for this artifact.

## Notes for the companion

**The honest framing.** Lead with what is not known: no study measures this document. Then give the reader
the thing that is well-established and genuinely useful, which is the convergent test for whether a strategy
is real. The evidence gap is not a weakness of the bundle, it is the bundle's most defensible claim, and it
is a *tested* negative.

**Load-bearing sections.** Diagnosis is the one that cannot be skipped: it is the section every source treats
as the difference between a strategy and a wish [1][2][9][39]. "What we are not doing" is the section that
makes the document usable, and it is the one most often missing [4][59][61][63].

**The sharpest teaching points.**
1. A strategy that could be a competitor's strategy unchanged is not a strategy [63].
2. If nothing in it made anyone uncomfortable, it is a wish list [63].
3. A plan allocates resources you control; a strategy bets on behaviour you do not [20].
4. Goals are not strategy [1], and OKRs are not strategy [34][58].
5. The roadmap is downstream. If the strategy document contains dates and features, it is a roadmap [30][31].

**Formats to ship.** Two qualify under the ADR 0028 rule (structurally distinct AND in circulation with a
named source): the **kernel** (diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action) [1], which is the default because
it is the most-cited and the most content-forcing; and the **one-pager** as a Playing-to-Win choice cascade
[44]. Ramp's seven-question template [50] is described in the guide as a real published instance rather than
shipped, because it is one company's document rather than a named reusable format. The Perri and Reforge
cascades [41][52] are a *placement* note, not a format: they describe a system of documents at different
altitudes, and this bundle occupies one rung of it.

**What the example must chain onto.** The Acme Analytics thread, upward from the PRD's FY26 "Time to
Insight" goal, per the strategy-docs contract.
